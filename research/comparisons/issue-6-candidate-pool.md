# Provision Project: Deep-Research Comparison of the Issue #6 Candidate Pool

## Research provenance and methodological frame

**Research platform:** ChatGPT  
**Model:** GPT-5.5 Thinking  
**Research mode:** Deep Research  
**Research performed:** September 28, 2026, America/Chicago  
**Controlling project artifact:** Provision Project Issue #6

Issue #6 defines the exercise as a **methodology and template stress test**, not a search for the five provisions with the largest fiscal cost, the most unusual apparent beneficiaries, or the strongest case for or against a tax policy. It also explicitly says that the five discovery buckets are not conclusions about economic incidence. [Issue #6](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6) The candidate pool analyzed below is therefore exactly the pool in the five Issue #6 comments: four agriculture candidates, four lower-income/worker candidates, four corporate/industry candidates, four educator candidates, and three wild cards. [Issue #6 — agriculture candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5791703840) [Issue #6 — lower-income/workers candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5829464785) [Issue #6 — corporate/industry candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5829604975) [Issue #6 — educator candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5829813673) [Issue #6 — wild-card candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5830037265)

The analysis applies the project's methodological separation among **current law; legal eligibility; observed use; direct tax benefit; distribution; documented legislative rationale; fiscal effect; and economic incidence**. The methodology expressly warns that the unit of analysis need not correspond to one Code section, that eligibility is not proof of use, that direct tax benefit does not establish economic incidence, and that “Unknown” is a legitimate result. [`docs/methodology.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/docs/methodology.md) The template institutionalizes the same distinctions in separate sections for scope, current law, eligibility, origins, observed use, direct benefit, incidence, distribution, fiscal effects, administration, uncertainty, source mapping, and project inference. [`research/provision-template.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/research/provision-template.md)

The project's source standards also matter to the conclusions below. Statutory claims are anchored where possible in current law and authoritative IRS/Treasury materials; enactment and rationale require Public Laws and contemporaneous legislative material; fiscal effects should preferentially use JCT/Treasury/CBO; and economic incidence requires empirical evidence rather than statutory characterization. [`docs/sources.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/docs/sources.md) That distinction is particularly important because Treasury defines a tax expenditure as a revenue loss attributable to a special exclusion, deduction, credit, preferential rate, or deferral and cautions that tax-expenditure estimates generally do **not** model behavioral responses and are **not** the same thing as revenue that repeal would raise. Timing provisions pose an additional measurement problem: present-value measures may be more informative than annual cash-flow tax-expenditure numbers. JCT's latest tax-expenditure report available in this research pass is JCX-45-25, covering fiscal years 2025–2029. Two discrepancies deserve to be surfaced before the matrix:

**Saver's Credit / Saver's Match citation.** Issue #6 labels the candidate “Saver's Credit / Saver's Match — 26 U.S.C. § 25B.” [Issue #6 — lower-income/workers candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5829464785) That is no longer a sufficient current-law citation. The Saver's **Match** is codified at **26 U.S.C. § 6433**, added by the SECURE 2.0 legislation in 2022 and applicable beginning with taxable years after 2026. [IRS Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-35_IRB) Current §6433 provides for a government matching contribution paid into the eligible individual's retirement savings vehicle. Thus the candidate is better understood in September 2026 as a **transition from the §25B retirement-savings credit to the new §6433 Saver's Match**, rather than as a single §25B provision. That makes it a stronger methodology test, but the Issue #6 citation should be corrected.

**The “large corporations” bucket contains broad business provisions.** The Research Credit and bonus depreciation are not legally confined to large corporations; the issue itself acknowledges that the Research Credit is broad rather than industry-specific and that bonus depreciation applies broadly. [Issue #6 — corporate/industry candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5829604975) That is not a reason to remove them—the category is expressly a discovery bucket—but a final record should not translate the bucket label into an eligibility or incidence claim.

The matrix reveals a useful feature of the candidate pool: the hardest cases are not necessarily those with complicated statutory text. Qualified tuition reductions, campus lodging, whaling expenses, and crop-insurance deferral are comparatively intelligible legal rules but difficult empirical records because observed-use and incidence evidence can disappear precisely where the tax system does not require a separately visible return line. By contrast, the EITC and LIHTC are administratively much more complicated but leave large official and empirical evidence trails. That difference directly exercises the methodology's instruction that a record can be complete even when a research question ends in “Unknown.” [`docs/methodology.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/docs/methodology.md)

## Candidate research notes

**Farm income averaging.** This is unusually attractive as a clean mechanism case. The modern rule is neither an exclusion nor a deduction: it changes the rates effectively applied to elected farm income by reference to prior years. Current IRS materials make its mechanics and filing procedure relatively tractable, while the historical problem is subtler: the current farm rule reused a section number previously associated with a broader income-averaging regime. A Provision record would therefore have to distinguish the ancestry of the **concept of income averaging**, the former general §1301 regime, and the enactment of the modern farm-specific rule in 1997. Its weakness as an MVP candidate is empirical: recent claimant and incidence evidence is much thinner than for household credits. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**Soil and water conservation expenditures.** Section 175 is more interesting than an ordinary “deduction” label suggests. Its counterfactual is capitalization, so part of the advantage may be accelerated recovery rather than a permanent exemption of income. IRS farm guidance also ties qualified treatment to conservation plans associated with NRCS or comparable state authorities, which means a complete record would have to leave Title 26. The provision is therefore a good combined test of timing, cross-agency rules, and limited usage data. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**Crop-insurance proceeds deferral.** Section 451(f) is the purest timing case in the pool. The statute permits a qualifying cash-method taxpayer to move recognition into the following year when the taxpayer normally would have reported income from the damaged crops then. This makes it methodologically useful even if its fiscal scale is modest: the research record would fail if it described the entire deferred amount as a permanent tax benefit or treated a one-year cash-flow estimate as equivalent to long-run revenue loss. Treasury's warning that present-value measurement is often more meaningful for deferrals fits this candidate almost perfectly. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**Conservation cost-sharing exclusion.** Section 126 supplies something the agriculture pool otherwise lacks: a genuine exclusion tied to an external government-program architecture. The statutory list includes multiple conservation/reclamation programs, so “who qualifies” cannot be answered solely from a tax return or a generic description of farmers. It is also likely to expose the project's observed-use section to a structural problem: amounts successfully excluded from income may be harder to observe in tax-return data than affirmative credits or deductions. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**EITC.** The EITC would establish a valuable benchmark for what a Provision record looks like when almost every evidence category is abundant. IRS data permit direct measurement of millions of claims and tens of billions of dollars of credits; the legislative history is deep; and empirical research studies labor-supply, take-up, compliance, wage effects and distribution. The danger is the opposite of the obscure candidates: a record could become unmanageably broad or silently collapse several different empirical findings into “who benefits.” Evidence that the EITC affects work or that part of its value is reflected in wages does not justify one universal incidence percentage. It is therefore a strong test of the template's bounded-incidence assessments.

**Child Tax Credit / Additional Child Tax Credit.** The CTC's value as a test comes principally from **legislative versioning**. Since 1997, statutory changes have altered amount, refundability, phase-ins, phaseouts, eligibility and temporary rules. A historical empirical estimate often describes a different CTC than the one in force today. The candidate therefore forces the source map to preserve the “law/provision version” field that the template requires. [`research/provision-template.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/research/provision-template.md) It overlaps the EITC in mechanism and data richness, however, so selecting both would sacrifice some mechanism diversity.

**Premium Tax Credit.** The PTC is substantially less duplicative than the EITC or CTC despite also being refundable. Its distinctive feature is **advance payment followed by tax reconciliation**, with legal and administrative machinery split between the Code and the ACA/Marketplace system. The 2026 return to a different subsidy schedule after temporary enhanced assistance expired also makes date-specific current-law work essential. It is an excellent cross-program and administration test, while incidence is intrinsically difficult because insurance premiums and market participation mediate the subsidy. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence for a single general conclusion.**

**Saver's Credit / Saver's Match.** This is the clearest candidate-pool correction. The upcoming Match is **§6433**, not merely §25B. Current Code text says the government match is payable into an eligible retirement savings vehicle after the taxpayer files a claim. Because the new system begins after 2026, a 2026 record would contain unusually asymmetric evidence: enacted statutory mechanics, legislative budget estimates and implementation guidance can be studied, but **actual §6433 take-up, realized distribution and behavioral effects do not yet exist**. That is an excellent test of whether the template can distinguish “not yet observable” from “researcher failed to find data.”

**Research Credit.** The credit's 1981 origin, multiple section-number changes, repeated statutory revisions, and long temporary-extension history make it a sophisticated legislative-history case. It also exposes a key incidence mistake to avoid: evidence that the credit induces additional R&D would be evidence about **behavior**, not necessarily about who captures the economic value. IRS substantiation guidance indicates that even current-law mechanics can require extensive factual inquiry. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence for a general allocation.**

**Bonus depreciation.** This is arguably the best business candidate for testing the project's fiscal-effect methodology. Current law again permits 100% first-year depreciation for qualifying post-January-2025 investment under the applicable rules. Because the alternative is generally depreciation over time, much of the federal-revenue effect is an acceleration of deductions rather than an exclusion from the tax base. Tax-expenditure estimates, short-run legislative scores, present-value estimates, and eventual cash receipts answer different questions. Treasury expressly highlights this issue for timing provisions. A strong record would make that distinction impossible for a reader to miss.

**Intangible drilling and development costs.** IDC is a useful counterweight to the modern large programs because it is old, industry-specific, and partly regulatory in operation. Section 263(c)'s history reaches to the 1954 Code, but the underlying treatment has administrative antecedents that should not automatically be called enactment of the exact modern provision. It therefore tests historical lineage in a different way from the distilled-spirits tax: not centuries of successive statutes, but the relationship between administrative tax practice, codification, regulations and subsequent statutory interactions. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**LIHTC.** LIHTC is the pool's most obvious case where the statutory claimant, policy target and potential economic beneficiaries are different actors. Issue #6 itself highlights investors, developers/intermediaries and low-income tenants as distinct parties whose shares of the subsidy should not be assumed. [Issue #6 — corporate/industry candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5829604975) Its federal-state allocation and long compliance structure also make it a demanding administration record. IRS regulatory material confirms the roles of property owners, tenants and housing-credit agencies. Even with a rich housing literature, the project should preserve bounded findings by market, period and provision version rather than force a single national incidence number.

**Educator-expense deduction.** The candidate is stronger in 2026 than its familiar “$300 teacher deduction” description implies. IRS guidance now describes additional itemized-deduction treatment beginning with 2026 expenses, creating a true interacting regime rather than a single capped line-item deduction. That makes §§62/67/162 a useful test of the project's definition of a provision as a related set of rules rather than one section. [`docs/methodology.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/docs/methodology.md) Its limitation is that it may not offer much credible economic-incidence evidence. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**Teacher Loan Forgiveness exclusion.** The tax exclusion and the education program must be treated as separate legal layers. Issue #6 is right to characterize the candidate as dependent on federal education law. [Issue #6 — educator candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5829813673) Current taxpayer-advocate material also confirms an important 2026 distinction: the temporary general federal exclusion for many student-loan discharges expired after 2025, but qualifying Teacher Loan Forgiveness continues to avoid federal income tax. The historical section would need multiple roots—one for the tax rule and another for the substantive loan program. That makes this a particularly valuable provision-boundary test. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**Qualified tuition reductions.** Current IRS guidance shows that §117(d) reaches qualifying educational-institution employees and family members and contains special graduate-level treatment for teaching/research roles. The modern rule traces to 1984 legislation. Its methodological value lies less in legal complexity than in empirical invisibility: an excluded employer benefit may leave much less usable tax-return evidence than a separately claimed credit. A record could therefore have “Established” current law but “Unknown” observed use and incidence without contradiction. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**Qualified campus lodging.** This is probably the strongest educator candidate for testing research on a genuinely obscure rule. IRS material describes special campus-lodging valuation, while the statute uses institution and academic-health-center definitions that reach beyond a simple employer/employee rule. The likely research bottleneck is not understanding the legal formula but finding credible data on who actually receives the benefit, its value, and any capitalization into compensation. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**Alaska Native subsistence whaling expenses.** Section 170(n)'s principal methodological value is evidence scarcity combined with extraordinary specificity. Issue #6 records the 2004 origin, the Alaska Eskimo Whaling Commission connection, and the charitable-contribution mechanism while correctly refusing to infer why Congress chose that mechanism. [Issue #6 — wild-card candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5830037265) Unlike EITC or LIHTC, the central test here may be whether the final record can honestly remain thin in several empirical sections while still being useful. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**Railroad Track Maintenance Credit.** Current §45G uses a 40% credit rate and a track-mile limitation; the statute also permits qualifying assignment of track miles, which means the taxpayer claiming the credit need not simply be equated with the infrastructure owner. IRS materials document its evolution from a temporary credit to a permanent one and the post-2022 rate. This makes it an unusually good tax-extender case and a good test of legal claimant versus affected market participants. **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence.**

**Federal distilled-spirits excise tax.** This candidate changes the nature of the project more than any other. It is a tax-imposing regime rather than a tax preference, so the fiscal section must measure receipts/revenue rather than “cost.” Its lineage also requires disciplined historical vocabulary: the 1791 whiskey tax is ancestry, not an uninterrupted statutory provision; TTB's historical series supports continuity of federal distilled-spirits taxation from 1862. [Issue #6 — wild-card candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5830037265) And §5001 alone does not capture the practical regime: reduced rates, §5010 credits, filing/removal rules, regulations and TTB administration all matter. It is therefore a particularly powerful test of the project's unit-of-analysis definition.

### What the nine-dimension mapping adds

This view makes several distinctions that the reconnaissance matrix previously compressed:

- **Eligibility is not economic population.** The Research Credit and bonus depreciation are broad business provisions even though they originated in the corporate/industry discovery bucket; conversely, LIHTC's legal claimant population and the lower-income tenant population implicated by the program are different concepts. Where the reconnaissance did not establish actual distribution, the table says so.
- **Statutory complexity is not administrative complexity.** A provision can be legally compact yet depend heavily on an external administrative system (§126), or be both legally and administratively complex (PTC and LIHTC). This distinction supports the report's later finding that cross-program dependency deserves explicit treatment.
- **Age is not merely the age of the current Code citation.** Farm income averaging, IDC, and distilled spirits illustrate different problems of predecessor rules, administrative ancestry, reused section numbers, and codification.
- **Fiscal scale remains the least complete dimension.** The table preserves that limitation rather than forcing observed claims, tax expenditures, legislative scores, present-value timing effects, and excise receipts onto one false common scale.

The result is a direct checklist against the nine Issue #6 criteria rather than a replacement for the richer candidate notes below. It should be used to assess **portfolio diversity**, not to score or rank individual tax policies.

## Cross-pool analysis

The pool is substantially more diverse in **mechanism** than the category names initially suggest. It includes refundable credits, a conventional business credit, an advanceable/reconciled credit, a future retirement-account match, deductions, exclusions, income averaging, one-year income deferral, accelerated cost recovery, special valuation, and an excise tax. Agriculture alone deliberately supplies four different mechanisms; the wild cards add both a highly specialized charitable deduction and a revenue-raising excise regime. [Issue #6 — agriculture candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5791703840) [Issue #6 — wild-card candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5830037265)

The **least well represented mechanism** is a preferential tax rate as such. The pool also contains no clean estate/gift-tax provision, international-tax provision, tax-exempt-organization rule, capital-gains realization rule, or entity-classification provision. Those are genuine blind spots in this pool, although they are not defects that must be fixed for the first five. By contrast, **credits are heavily represented**: all four lower-income candidates are credit/subsidy systems in either their present or imminent form, three corporate/wild candidates are credits, and LIHTC adds another elaborate credit. Choosing EITC/CTC plus Research Credit/LIHTC plus §45G could therefore produce a five-record portfolio that looks demographically diverse while being methodologically credit-heavy. The issue's own diversity objective argues against mistaking demographic variety for mechanism variety. [Issue #6](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6)

**Exclusions are present but empirically difficult.** Section 126, Teacher Loan Forgiveness, qualified tuition reductions and campus lodging all involve excluded income or special exclusion/valuation treatment. These are valuable precisely because tax-return data may provide less direct observed-use information than it does for affirmative credits. That creates a useful counterweight to the evidence-rich EITC, CTC, PTC, Research Credit and LIHTC.

**Timing is represented unusually well.** Crop-insurance deferral is a narrow one-year recognition rule; §175 and IDC alter capitalization/current-deduction timing; and bonus depreciation accelerates capital-cost recovery on a very large scale. These are related but not duplicative stress tests. Crop-insurance deferral offers conceptual clarity, §175 adds a conservation-program dependency, IDC adds old industry-specific history/regulatory delegation, and bonus depreciation provides rich empirical/fiscal evidence plus potentially very large short-run revenue effects. Treasury's distinction between cash-flow tax expenditures and present-value treatment makes these candidates particularly important for testing the fiscal-effect section. The pool also spans a useful **evidence-availability gradient**. EITC, CTC, PTC, Research Credit, bonus depreciation and LIHTC have abundant official and empirical material. At the opposite end, whaling expenses, campus lodging, qualified tuition reductions and several agriculture provisions appear likely to end with important Unknowns in observed use or incidence. A method tested only against the first group might inadvertently become dependent on unusually rich evidence; a method tested only against the second might never exercise its distributional and empirical-study fields.

The strongest **economic-incidence stress tests** are LIHTC, PTC, Research Credit, bonus depreciation and §45G. LIHTC is especially valuable because tax investor, developer, property owner, landowner and tenant can be distinct; PTC introduces insurers and health-market price responses; the Research Credit raises worker/owner/spillover questions; bonus depreciation introduces capital-market and investment response; and §45G's statutory assignment rules can separate claimant from track ownership. [Issue #6 — corporate/industry candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5829604975) None justifies an incidence conclusion from the claimant's identity.

The EITC is different: it has one of the richest incidence-relevant literatures in the pool. Evidence of labor-supply responses and some employer capture through wages means the project can actually test how to summarize empirical incidence findings without pretending they establish an invariant division of the credit. That makes EITC useful not because incidence is “easy,” but because there is enough evidence to test the template's evidence-state and disagreement machinery.

The strongest **legislative-history tests** are also diverse. Farm income averaging tests reuse of a Code section after a previous regime was repealed. The Research Credit and §45G test repeated temporary extensions. CTC tests repeated substantive redesign. Saver's Match tests an enacted future regime replacing a familiar present one. IDC tests administrative ancestry versus statutory codification. Distilled spirits tests centuries of ancestry while demanding a distinction between the 1791 ancestor and continuous post-1862 taxation. The strongest **cross-program or cross-agency dependencies** are §126, PTC, LIHTC, Teacher Loan Forgiveness and—less conventionally—§170(n). These involve conservation programs/USDA, ACA Marketplaces/CMS, state housing-credit agencies, Department of Education loan programs, or the Alaska Eskimo Whaling Commission framework, respectively. PTC and LIHTC are administrative-system tests; Teacher Loan Forgiveness is a provision-boundary test because the substantive benefit is created outside the tax code. [Issue #6 — educator candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5829813673)

There are also **methodological duplicates**, depending on which five are chosen. EITC and CTC both provide large, evidence-rich family credits and substantial legislative history, although the EITC is stronger on work incentives and CTC on versioning. Research Credit and §45G both give business-credit history, but §45G is narrower and better for extender/assignment questions. Crop-insurance deferral and bonus depreciation both test timing, but at radically different scale and evidence richness. Qualified tuition reduction and campus lodging are both education-employment exclusions with weak use data, although campus lodging adds valuation and greater obscurity. LIHTC and PTC both combine tax subsidies with a larger non-tax policy system; selecting both would strongly test cross-program administration but leave less room for obscure or historical rules.

The largest structural blind spot is therefore **not** “too few obscure provisions”: whaling, campus lodging and several agricultural rules solve that. Nor is it “too few timing rules”: the pool has several. The actual imbalance is toward **income-tax preferences and credits**, with only one tax-imposing excise regime and no clean preferential-rate or international/entity/estate-tax candidate. That makes distilled spirits uniquely valuable if the maintainers want to learn whether the current notion of a “provision” and the fiscal template work outside tax expenditures.

## Methodology and template stress test

**The definition of a provision is directionally right but will be forced to make hard boundary choices.** The methodology says the unit is a distinct rule or related set of rules rather than necessarily a Code section. [`docs/methodology.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/docs/methodology.md) The candidate pool validates that design, but it also exposes several cases where “related set” can expand indefinitely. The educator-expense regime spans §§62/67/162; Research Credit mechanics interact with multiple R&E rules; LIHTC is intelligible only with allocation/compliance machinery; and “§5001 et seq.” can become almost the entire distilled-spirits excise regime. A record needs an explicit principle for when a cross-reference is **inside the provision boundary** versus merely a **related provision**. The template asks researchers to state boundaries, but these candidates will reveal whether that instruction is sufficiently operational. [`research/provision-template.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/research/provision-template.md)

**The template handles multiple historical roots better than a single-origin model, and several candidates will test that feature immediately.** Teacher Loan Forgiveness has a tax-law root and a substantive education-program root. Distilled spirits has historical ancestry, repeated statutory regimes, continuous taxation from 1862, and a modern codification that are not the same historical proposition. Farm income averaging reuses a Code section formerly associated with a different policy. IDC may require distinguishing administrative practice from statutory codification. The template's “Origin / predecessor entry” and “Historical boundary note” fields are therefore essential rather than optional refinements. [`research/provision-template.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/research/provision-template.md)

**Current-law snapshots need explicit as-of dates and future-effective-law treatment.** Saver's Match demonstrates the problem vividly: in September 2026, §6433 is enacted current law but its operational regime begins in 2027. The PTC underwent a material 2026 change when temporary enhanced subsidies ended; bonus depreciation changed materially in 2025; educator-expense treatment changed in 2026. “Current law” therefore needs to distinguish at least **enacted**, **currently operative**, and **future-effective** rules. The template has effective-date fields, but this candidate pool will test whether the public presentation makes those distinctions visible enough.

**Observed-use fields work very differently for affirmative claims and exclusions.** EITC provides an explicit claimed credit and large IRS data series. Qualified tuition reductions, campus lodging, §126 and Teacher Loan Forgiveness may produce little or no separately identifiable return-line evidence precisely because the income is excluded. A template that implicitly expects “number of taxpayers using the provision” for every record could create false incompleteness. The existing “What can the data not establish?” field is well designed for this problem, but the first records should test whether “not observable in tax data” can be represented distinctly from “not researched.” [`research/provision-template.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/research/provision-template.md)

**The separation of direct benefit and economic incidence is essential and should survive contact with evidence-rich cases.** LIHTC, PTC, §45G and the Research Credit all make the legal-claimant problem obvious. EITC is subtler because the claimant directly receives a refundable credit, yet empirical wage responses can still affect ultimate incidence. The candidate pool supports the methodology's decision to let an incidence section conclude **Unknown** even when direct tax benefit is measurable with high confidence. [`docs/methodology.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/docs/methodology.md)

**Fiscal-effect fields need to accommodate fundamentally different measurement objects.** For EITC, an IRS total of credits claimed is observed use/direct benefit, not necessarily a repeal score. For bonus depreciation and crop-insurance deferral, timing makes cash-flow and present-value figures materially different. For a newly enacted redesign such as Saver's Match, the best evidence may initially be a legislative revenue estimate rather than observed cost. For distilled spirits, the measure is positive tax receipts, not tax-expenditure loss. Treasury expressly warns against equating tax expenditures with repeal revenue and against naïve treatment of timing provisions. The template already records estimate type, period, baseline, behavior, interactions and timing convention; these candidates will test whether the eventual data model can compare records without flattening those fields into a single “cost” number. [`research/provision-template.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/research/provision-template.md)

**Administrative complexity may deserve a more explicit “external system” concept.** The current Administration and Implementation section can describe agencies and forms, but PTC, LIHTC, §126 and Teacher Loan Forgiveness are not merely tax rules with some extra guidance: their practical eligibility or operation depends on an external program architecture. [`research/provision-template.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/research/provision-template.md) A useful finding for maintainers is therefore that “administration” has at least two qualitatively different forms in the candidate pool: **tax-administration complexity** such as EITC substantiation or Research Credit records, and **cross-program dependency** such as CMS Exchanges, state housing agencies or Department of Education forgiveness.

**Sparse evidence should be treated as a successful test outcome, not a failed record.** Whaling expenses, campus lodging and possibly several agricultural provisions may never support a confident economic-incidence conclusion. That is consistent with the methodology's declared evidence states and completion rule: an initially complete record may contain Unknowns when the relevant evidence has actually been investigated. [`docs/methodology.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/docs/methodology.md) For those cases, the correct result is:

**Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence**

rather than a narrative assumption based on the legal claimant.

**Mechanism-maximizing portfolio.** Farm income averaging supplies a rare smoothing mechanism; PTC supplies an advanceable/reconciled refundable credit; bonus depreciation supplies accelerated cost recovery; §117(d) supplies an exclusion; and distilled spirits supplies excise taxation. [Issue #6 — wild-card candidates](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6#issuecomment-5830037265) This portfolio would most directly answer whether the methodology works both when the literature is overwhelming and when the honest answer to multiple template fields is Unknown. It under-tests cross-agency social-program administration compared with a PTC/Teacher Loan Forgiveness portfolio, and it lacks the exceptionally long history of distilled spirits.

**Boundary-and-administration portfolio.** Section 126 depends on conservation programs; PTC depends on ACA Exchanges/CMS; LIHTC depends on state allocation and project-compliance systems; Teacher Loan Forgiveness depends on a substantive federal education program outside Title 26; and §45G permits track-mile assignments that can separate claimant from infrastructure ownership. This would be the most aggressive test of “what belongs inside a provision record?” and of cross-agency administration. The price is mechanism concentration: PTC, LIHTC and §45G are all credits/subsidies, and the portfolio does not test excise taxation or a major timing provision.

**History-and-versioning portfolio.** Farm income averaging tests an old Code-section identity reused for a modern provision; Saver's Match tests a policy currently crossing from §25B to a future-effective §6433 architecture; Research Credit tests decades of redesign and former temporary extensions; educator-expense treatment tests a multi-section rule changed again in 2026; distilled spirits tests ancestry versus continuous taxation versus modern codification. This is the strongest portfolio for the historical-development and source-provenance portions of the template. Its weakness is observed-use comparability: new §6433 has no realized use yet, while distilled spirits is a tax rather than a benefit, so cross-record quantitative comparison would be intentionally difficult.

**Incidence-uncertainty portfolio.** Section 175 asks whether a conservation-linked deduction affects behavior beyond the claimant; EITC brings unusually strong empirical labor/wage evidence; LIHTC provides a multi-party housing subsidy; tuition reductions raise compensation/capitalization questions with poor direct data; and §45G separates claimant, track owner and downstream rail users. This portfolio would put the methodology's most important conceptual safeguard—**legal eligibility is not economic incidence**—under maximum pressure. Its weakness is that four of the five may ultimately produce bounded or Unknown incidence conclusions, and it under-tests ancient legislative history and excise-tax fiscal treatment.

No portfolio dominates the others because they answer different questions about the project itself. The mechanism-maximizing portfolio asks whether the template is flexible across tax instruments. The evidence-gradient portfolio asks whether it works under radically different information conditions. The boundary portfolio asks what a “provision” actually is. The history portfolio asks whether the project can reconstruct changing policy identities over time. The incidence portfolio asks whether it can resist unsupported beneficiary claims even when a record would feel more satisfying with a definitive answer. That is exactly the kind of tradeoff Issue #6 says the initial five are supposed to expose. [Issue #6](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6)

## Verification and durable-source pass

A follow-up verification pass was performed after the original Deep Research run because the exported report contained ChatGPT-internal citation references that were not usable outside the research session. Those internal references have been replaced with durable links to the Provision Project artifacts they cited. The pass also independently checked material current-law and mechanics claims against official sources.

The following consequential claims survived that check:

- **Saver’s Match:** §6433 was added by SECURE 2.0 and applies for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2026; it replaces the §25B Saver’s Credit for the covered retirement contributions. See [IRS Notice 2026-48](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-35_IRB) and the [IRS Saver’s Match overview](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/savers-match).
- **Educator expenses:** IRS guidance confirms that educator expenses may also be deducted as itemized deductions beginning in 2026. See [IRS educational-assistance FAQ](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/updates-to-frequently-asked-questions-about-educational-assistance-programs).
- **Bonus depreciation:** IRS Notice 2026-11 confirms the permanent 100% additional first-year depreciation deduction for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025, subject to the statutory and transition rules described in the notice. See [Notice 2026-11](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-06_IRB).
- **EITC observed use:** IRS Statistics of Income reports approximately 23 million TY2023 returns with about $64 billion of EITC. See [IRS EITC statistics](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/individuals/earned-income-tax-credit/earned-income-tax-credit-statistics).
- **Farm income averaging mechanics:** Schedule J permits eligible farming/fishing income to be averaged over the prior three base years. See [Schedule J instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sj).
- **Crop-insurance deferral mechanics:** §451(f) permits qualifying cash-method taxpayers to elect following-year inclusion when the statutory practice test is met. See [26 U.S.C. §451](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A26+section%3A451+edition%3Aprelim%29) and [Schedule F instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sf).
- **§175 mechanics:** current law permits a taxpayer engaged in farming to deduct qualifying soil/water-conservation and endangered-species-recovery expenditures, subject to the statutory limitation. See [26 U.S.C. §175](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A26+section%3A175+edition%3Aprelim%29).
- **§126 mechanics:** current law ties the exclusion to qualifying conservation programs and statutory determinations. See [26 U.S.C. §126](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A26+section%3A126+edition%3Aprelim%29).
- **Research Credit:** current §41 is not limited to large corporations, and IRS historical guidance confirms that the credit originated as §44F in the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981. See [26 U.S.C. §41](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A26+section%3A41+edition%3Aprelim%29) and the [IRS Research Credit audit guide](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/audit-techniques-guide-credit-for-increasing-research-activities-ie-research-tax-credit-irc-ss41-introduction).
- **PTC mechanics:** IRS guidance confirms that the Premium Tax Credit is refundable, may be advanced through a Marketplace to the insurer, and is reconciled on Form 8962. See the [IRS PTC overview](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/premium-tax-credit-ptc-overview).
- **Qualified tuition reductions:** IRS guidance confirms the §117(d) exclusion and its principal employee/family and graduate-level limitations. See [IRS Qualified Tuition Reduction guidance](https://www.irs.gov/government-entities/federal-state-local-governments/qualified-tuition-reduction).
- **Qualified campus lodging:** current §119(d) contains the educational-institution lodging exclusion and valuation rule described in the matrix. See [26 U.S.C. §119](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title26-section119).
- **Railroad Track Maintenance Credit:** current §45G provides a 40% credit rate for qualifying expenditures for tax years beginning after 2022; IRS instructions state that the credit was made permanent for tax years beginning after 2022. See [26 U.S.C. §45G](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A26+section%3A45G+edition%3Aprelim%29) and [Form 8900 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8900).
- **Distilled-spirits tax:** current §5001 imposes a $13.50-per-proof-gallon federal tax before any applicable reduced-rate provisions elsewhere in the regime. See [26 U.S.C. §5001](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title26-chapter51).

## Open questions, limitations, and repository submission

The most important unresolved research issue is **current comparable fiscal scale**. This reconnaissance established the measurement framework and located current JCT and Treasury tax-expenditure resources, including JCT's FY2025–2029 report and Treasury's FY2027 tables. It did not extract and verify a current row-level dollar amount for every one of the nineteen candidates. That is deliberate: many candidates are not separately reported; exclusions can be embedded in broader categories; distilled spirits requires a receipts measure rather than a tax-expenditure measure; timing provisions may require present-value treatment; and observed claimed amounts such as the EITC's roughly $64 billion for TY2023 are not interchangeable with tax-expenditure or repeal-revenue estimates. A selected provision record should therefore capture the exact JCT/Treasury table row or legislative score and all the template's required metadata rather than inherit a loose number from this comparison.

Several **origin questions also remain intentionally open** at reconnaissance depth: the precise original enactment and predecessor chain for crop-insurance deferral; the full pre-codification ancestry of IDC; and the exact enactment-era record for campus lodging deserve Public Law/Statutes at Large reconstruction before being stated as Established. This follows, rather than violates, the project's rule that a modern Code citation should not be mistaken for a policy's true historical origin. [`docs/methodology.md`](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/blob/main/docs/methodology.md)

The most consequential **candidate correction is established**, however: the Issue #6 Saver's Match citation should not remain simply §25B. The Match is §6433, enacted in 2022 and effective for post-2026 taxable years. The most consequential **current-law update** is the educator-expense regime's 2026 itemized-deduction treatment, which makes that candidate more clearly a multi-section regime than a simple above-the-line deduction. Bonus depreciation also needs to be researched under the post-2025 permanent 100% regime rather than an older phase-down description.
