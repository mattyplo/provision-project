# Provision Project — EITC (§32) Deep Research Prompt

**Prompt version:** EITC 1.0 (adapted from standard prompt v1.0)  
**Purpose:** Source-backed research into a provision of the U.S. federal tax code  
**Project:** The Provision Project  
**Research mode:** Deep Research

---

## 1. Research Assignment

You are conducting comprehensive legal, historical, fiscal, and economic research for the Provision Project, an open-source project documenting provisions of the United States federal tax code.

The project's central questions are:

1. What does this provision do?
2. Who legally qualifies for it?
3. When and how did it enter federal tax law?
4. Why was it created?
5. How has it changed over time?
6. Who actually uses it?
7. Who receives the direct tax benefit?
8. Who ultimately benefits economically, and what evidence supports that conclusion?
9. What is its estimated fiscal effect?
10. How confident can we be in each answer?

The objective is to produce a comprehensive, source-backed research report that can support the creation of a publicly accessible provision record.

This is an evidence-gathering exercise, not an argument for or against the provision.

Do not characterize the provision as a loophole, subsidy, giveaway, abuse, or otherwise make normative judgments unless describing and attributing a specific source's characterization.

The project does not require a definitive conclusion where evidence is insufficient.

An explicit finding of **Unknown** is preferable to an unsupported conclusion.

### Provision to research

- **Working name:** Federal Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)
- **Primary Code citation:** 26 U.S.C. §32
- **Jurisdiction:** United States federal tax law
- **Current-law as-of date:** October 31, 2026
- **Historical coverage:** From the earliest identifiable statutory origin or recognizable predecessor through the current-law as-of date
- **Research scope:** The federal EITC under §32, including its predecessors, refundability, earned-income and investment-income rules, qualifying-child and childless-worker rules, phase-in/plateau/phase-out calculations, filing and administration, observed claims and take-up, fiscal estimates, behavioral effects, and economic incidence. Examine related Code provisions only as needed to explain §32; state EITCs and other transfer programs are contextual comparators, not independent research subjects.

### EITC-specific research requirements

- Trace the **original enactment in the 1970s** and investigate contemporaneous rationales (including payroll-tax relief, work incentives, and income support) without presuming any one rationale is definitive. Verify statutory origin and later expansions against primary sources.
- Provide an intelligible, dated explanation of the **phase-in, maximum-credit plateau, and phase-out**; distinguish tax year 2026 parameters from earlier years and from the publication dates of IRS data.
- Investigate **2025–2026 enacted changes** where relevant. Distinguish enacted, operative, future-effective, and transitional rules using statutory effective-date provisions, rather than relying on undated summaries.
- Examine qualifying children, taxpayers without qualifying children, earned and investment income, filing status, residency, identification rules, refundability, disallowance, and compliance administration. Verify the governing Code/regulatory provisions.
- For observed use, separate **claims filed, claims allowed, number of taxpayers, credit dollars, refunds/outlays, take-up estimates, and improper-payment estimates**. Identify tax year versus fiscal year, denominators, revisions, and measurement uncertainty.
- For fiscal effects, search JCT and Treasury tax expenditure publications, CBO and federal budget materials; distinguish estimates of revenue loss, outlays, and the revenue effect of hypothetical policy changes.
- Evaluate labor supply (extensive and intensive margins), wages, employer incidence, family outcomes, and distribution using empirical literature; identify study populations, periods, identification strategies, and disagreements. Do not equate a causal estimate from one setting with a universal effect.
- Include state EITCs and interactions with other programs only where necessary to interpret the federal EITC, clearly outside the main provision boundary.

### Provision boundaries

The unit of analysis is the substantive tax provision, not necessarily the entire Code section or every program related to it.

Begin by investigating and establishing the precise boundaries of the provision.

Identify:

- The tax treatment being studied.
- The transactions, payments, activities, and taxpayers covered.
- Related Code sections necessary to understand its operation.
- Related provisions that should remain outside the research boundary.
- External government programs that materially affect eligibility, administration, or implementation.

Do not assume the preliminary working scope is legally complete or accurate.

Where the provision's boundaries are ambiguous, explain the ambiguity and recommend a defensible research boundary.

Do not silently expand the study to encompass state EITCs, the Child Tax Credit, SNAP, TANF, minimum-wage policy, or antipoverty policy generally. Discuss interactions only where material to federal §32.

## 2. Research Standards

### Source hierarchy

Prioritize primary and nonpartisan official sources.

Preferred sources include:

**Statutory and legislative sources**
- United States Code
- Public Laws and Statutes at Large
- Congressional committee reports
- Conference reports
- Joint Committee on Taxation publications
- Congressional legislative materials
- Official historical statutory compilations

**Administrative and government sources**
- Treasury regulations
- Internal Revenue Service guidance, publications, forms, and instructions
- Treasury Department reports
- Congressional Budget Office publications
- Government Accountability Office reports
- Official government administrative data
- Relevant federal agency documentation

**Economic and historical research**
- Peer-reviewed academic research
- National Bureau of Economic Research working papers
- Credible university and research-institution publications
- Other methodologically transparent empirical research

**Supplementary sources**
- Professional tax commentary
- Industry publications
- Historical accounts
- Journalism
- Stakeholder and advocacy materials

Secondary sources may help identify primary documents, explain technical concepts, or present empirical findings.

However, do not rely on secondary summaries when the relevant primary source can reasonably be obtained.

### Source provenance

For consequential claims, provide:

- Source title
- Author or issuing institution
- Publication date
- Document identifier, where applicable
- Stable URL or document locator
- Relevant page, section, table, paragraph, or statutory subsection
- Whether the source is primary or secondary
- Whether the source is contemporaneous or retrospective, when relevant
- Whether the source directly supports the claim or provides contextual evidence

Do not invent citations, quotations, page numbers, URLs, document identifiers, or statistical estimates.

Do not present a source as directly inspected unless its contents were actually accessed.

If a source is identified but cannot be retrieved, label it **Identified but not verified**.

If a source is inaccessible, explain the limitation.

### Citation accuracy

A citation must support the specific claim to which it is attached.

Distinguish:

- **Direct support:** The source directly supports the claim.
- **Contextual support:** The source provides relevant context but is insufficient by itself.
- **Lead only:** The source identifies potentially relevant evidence requiring independent verification.
- **Inference support:** The source supplies evidence used in an explicitly identified analytical inference.

Do not treat an AI-generated summary as independent evidence of the underlying claim.

### Evidence states

Use the following evidence states when assessing findings:

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Established | Direct, authoritative evidence establishes the finding with no material unresolved qualification |
| Supported | Credible evidence supports the finding, subject to stated limitations |
| Uncertain | Evidence permits a tentative assessment but important uncertainty remains |
| Disputed | Credible sources reach materially conflicting conclusions |
| Unknown | Available evidence does not support a conclusion |

These are working descriptions; apply the project's methodology when making final classifications.

Use **Unknown** when evidence is insufficient. Explain the gap rather than inventing a result.

Do not substitute a numerical confidence score for an evidence-based explanation.

## 3. Required Research

Organize the research report according to the following sections.

### 3.1 Provision Definition and Scope

Establish:

- The provision's substantive mechanism.
- The primary statutory authority.
- Related statutory provisions.
- The precise research boundary.
- Important exclusions from the research boundary.
- Relevant external-program dependencies.

Explain why the selected boundary represents a coherent unit of tax policy.

Identify any unresolved boundary questions.

### 3.2 Current Law

Establish the law as of the specified research date.

Distinguish explicitly:

1. Law enacted as of that date.
2. Law currently operative.
3. Enacted provisions not yet operative.
4. Applicable effective dates.
5. Sunset or expiration provisions.
6. Transition rules.

Do not conflate enacted law with operative law.

Explain:

- The tax mechanism.
- Eligibility requirements.
- Elections and election procedures.
- Applicable limitations and exceptions.
- Applicable income definitions and household/filing-status rules.
- Credit computation, refundability, and timing rules.
- Interactions with other tax provisions.
- Important Treasury regulations and IRS guidance.

Provide statutory subsection citations and authoritative support for material timing rules.

Where material, distinguish the statute from administrative interpretation and judicial construction.

### 3.3 Legal Eligibility

Identify precisely who may claim or use the provision.

Explain:

- Eligible taxpayers.
- Eligible transactions or payments.
- Required circumstances.
- Important restrictions.
- Taxpayer elections.
- Relevant filing or reporting requirements, qualifying-child tests, age and residency rules, and taxpayer identification requirements.
- Situations in which the credit is unavailable.

Where useful, provide a hypothetical example illustrating the mechanics.

Label hypothetical examples as illustrative rather than observed taxpayer behavior.

Do not infer actual utilization from legal eligibility.

### 3.4 Origin and Enactment

Trace the provision to its earliest identifiable statutory origin or recognizable predecessor.

Identify, where available:

- Original legislation.
- Public Law number.
- Bill number.
- Enactment date.
- Statutes at Large citation.
- Original Code section.
- Original statutory language.
- Effective date.
- Important predecessor provisions.

Do not assume the current Code section marks the beginning of the policy.

If multiple statutory roots exist, document them separately.

Distinguish established historical lineage from uncertain or inferred connections.

### 3.5 Why Was the Provision Created?

Investigate the contemporaneous legislative rationale.

Prioritize:

- Committee reports.
- Conference reports.
- Contemporaneous JCT explanations.
- Treasury or administration materials.
- Relevant congressional records.

Identify the specific policy problems lawmakers or other participants said the provision would address.

Distinguish:

**Documented contemporaneous rationale:** Explanations recorded during enactment.

**Later interpretation:** Explanations offered after enactment by government agencies, historians, economists, or other researchers.

**Project inference:** A conclusion derived from evidence but not directly stated by an authoritative source.

Attribute explanations to the actual institution or speaker.

Do not automatically treat an individual legislator's statement as the intent of Congress as a whole.

If the original rationale cannot be established, say so.

### 3.6 Material Development History

Construct a chronological history of consequential developments.

Include:

- Statutory amendments.
- Changes to eligibility.
- Changes to timing or recognition rules.
- Changes to elections.
- Significant regulations.
- Material administrative guidance.
- Significant judicial interpretations.
- Important changes in implementation.

For each development, identify:

| Field | Required information |
|---|---|
| Date | Enactment or issuance date |
| Authority | Public Law, regulation, guidance, or decision |
| Change | What materially changed |
| Rationale | Documented explanation, if available |
| Effective date | When the change became operative |
| Fiscal effect | Contemporaneous estimate, if available |
| Sources | Supporting citations |

Distinguish statutory amendments from administrative interpretations.

Do not present proposed regulations as operative law.

Do not invent explanations for legislative changes where no documented rationale was found.

### 3.7 Observed Use

Investigate whether data exist showing actual utilization.

Search for:

- Number of taxpayers using the provision.
- Number of EITC claimants, claims, or returns (distinguish units).
- Total EITC amounts claimed, paid, or allowed (distinguish measures).
- Relevant IRS administrative statistics.
- IRS Statistics of Income and EITC administrative data.
- Government studies.
- Historical utilization estimates.
- Distribution across taxpayer or business categories.

For every statistic, identify:

- Measure.
- Value.
- Tax year or period.
- Population.
- Unit of observation.
- Source.
- Limitations.

Distinguish:

1. The number of taxpayers legally eligible.
2. The number actually using the provision.
3. The total credit claimed, allowed, or paid.
4. The nonrefundable offset and refundable portion of the credit, where separately measurable.

These are not interchangeable.

If no reliable utilization data are located, document the sources searched and explain the remaining evidence gap.

Do not infer EITC take-up or claim counts from poverty, employment, or eligibility statistics without clearly labeling and justifying the inference.

### 3.8 Direct Tax Benefit

Identify who receives the immediate tax treatment.

Explain:

- How the treatment changes federal income-tax liability or its timing.
- Which taxpayers receive that treatment directly.
- Whether available data quantify the direct benefit.
- Whether the benefit varies across taxpayer categories.

Distinguish nonrefundable tax liability offsets from refundable credit payments, and distinguish statutory credit amounts from realized after-tax gains.

Do not assume total credits claimed equal tax expenditures, program outlays, or ultimate economic gains.

Do not equate the direct tax recipient with the ultimate economic beneficiary.

### 3.9 Economic Incidence

Investigate who ultimately captures the economic value of the provision.

Look for credible empirical or theoretical research examining:

- Behavioral responses.
- Changes in prices or production.
- Capitalization effects.
- Effects on workers, employers, wages, labor supply, and household members, where empirically studied.
- Market structure.
- Distribution of economic gains.
- Other plausible channels supported by evidence.

Do not assume these effects exist merely because they are economically plausible.

For relevant studies, report:

- Population studied.
- Period studied.
- Version of the provision.
- Research methodology.
- Outcomes measured.
- Important assumptions.
- Limitations.
- Credible challenges or contradictory findings.

Distinguish empirical research on the federal EITC from evidence on other wage subsidies, state EITCs, or general antipoverty policies.

Evidence about wage subsidies or employment programs generally does not automatically establish the economic incidence of the federal EITC.

Where evidence supports only a narrow conclusion, preserve that limitation.

If credible evidence does not establish economic incidence, report:

**Economic incidence: Unknown**

Explain what evidence would be needed to reach a stronger conclusion.

### 3.10 Distribution

Investigate the distribution of:

- Legal eligibility.
- Observed use.
- Reported amounts receiving the tax treatment.
- Direct tax benefits.
- Economic benefits.

Consider earnings, filing status, number of qualifying children, family structure, geography, and other relevant categories where evidence exists.

Always identify which concept is being measured.

Do not treat eligibility, poverty counts, or total credit claims as a demonstrated distribution of ultimate economic benefits.

### 3.11 Estimated Fiscal Effect

Identify official estimates of the provision's federal revenue effect.

Search Treasury, JCT, CBO, and other appropriate government sources.

For each estimate, record:

- Estimating organization.
- Publication.
- Publication date.
- Estimate type.
- Law or proposal version.
- Years covered.
- Amount.
- Annual figure or budget-window total.
- Measurement basis and timing convention.
- Baseline.
- Treatment of behavioral responses.
- Interactions with other provisions.
- Methodological limitations.

Distinguish:

- Tax expenditure estimates.
- Legislative revenue estimates.
- Repeal or reform estimates.
- Observed tax collections.
- Income amounts deferred.
- Present-value measures.
- Timing effects.

Do not assume a tax expenditure estimate equals the revenue Congress would raise by repealing the provision.

For this refundable credit, investigate how tax expenditure and outlay components are recorded, including differences among JCT, Treasury, IRS, and budget presentations.

If no separately identifiable official estimate exists, explain that finding and document the search.

Do not manufacture a fiscal estimate.

### 3.12 Administration and Implementation

Identify the relevant administrative framework.

Investigate:

- Treasury regulations.
- IRS forms and instructions.
- Revenue rulings.
- Revenue procedures.
- Other IRS guidance.
- Filing requirements.
- Election procedures.
- Compliance issues.
- Material litigation.
- GAO or other administrative evaluations.

Distinguish statutory requirements from administrative implementation.

#### External-program dependencies

Identify whether operation of the tax provision materially depends on a program outside federal tax administration.

For each material dependency, document:

- Agency or program.
- External legal authority.
- Role in eligibility.
- Role in administration or delivery.
- Data or determinations generated.
- Relationship to the tax provision.
- Whether the dependency is constitutive or merely related context.

Do not treat the entire external program as part of the tax provision merely because it is related.

### 3.13 Significant Disagreements and Uncertainty

Identify material disagreements concerning:

- Statutory interpretation.
- Historical origins.
- Legislative rationale.
- Utilization.
- Fiscal estimates.
- Economic effects.
- Distribution.
- Economic incidence.

For each disagreement, explain:

- Competing conclusions.
- Sources supporting each position.
- Differences in assumptions or methodology.
- What evidence would help resolve the disagreement.

Attribute stakeholder arguments.

Do not manufacture balance where evidence overwhelmingly supports one conclusion.

Do not suppress credible disagreement merely to produce a simpler narrative.

### 3.14 Source Map

Produce a claim-to-source map for consequential findings.

Use the following structure:

| Claim ID | Claim | Source | Source role | Source status | Claim relationship | Pinpoint citation | Verification status | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Use source roles such as:

- Statute
- Regulation
- Administrative guidance
- Judicial interpretation
- Legislative history
- Government estimate
- Government data
- Government analysis
- Empirical research
- Secondary synthesis
- Stakeholder argument
- Journalism

Preserve source provenance and distinguish it from the source's relationship to the claim.

Identify sources that were directly examined and those that remain research leads.

A bibliography alone is insufficient.

### 3.15 Project Inferences

Identify consequential analytical conclusions that are not directly stated by a source.

For each inference, report:

- Conclusion.
- Supporting evidence.
- Reasoning.
- Evidence state.
- Limitations.
- What evidence could change the assessment.

Do not disguise an inference as an established statutory or historical fact.

### 3.16 Open Questions

List unresolved questions and research gaps.

Include:

- Missing historical documents.
- Unverified citations.
- Unavailable administrative data.
- Unresolved legal interpretations.
- Missing fiscal estimates.
- Economic-incidence questions.
- Important sources requiring additional investigation.

Prioritize questions by their importance to the provision record.

Distinguish a question that was investigated but remains unresolved from one that was not investigated.

### 3.17 Preliminary Source Audit

Perform a preliminary audit of the research report.

Evaluate whether:

- Provision boundaries are explicit.
- Current law has a dated snapshot.
- Operative and future-effective law are distinguished.
- Material legislative origins are supported.
- Legislative rationale is attributed.
- Eligibility and utilization remain separate.
- Direct tax benefits and economic incidence remain separate.
- Fiscal estimates are accurately characterized.
- Consequential claims have appropriate citations.
- Citations support their associated claims.
- Primary sources were used where reasonably obtainable.
- Unverified sources are clearly identified.
- Inferences are labeled.
- Important uncertainty is visible.

Report deficiencies explicitly.

Do not claim that the Provision Project's final source audit has been completed. That audit will be performed independently after the research reports have been reviewed and underlying sources verified.

### 3.18 Research Completion Summary

Conclude with four categories:

**Established findings**

Findings supported by direct, authoritative evidence.

**Supported findings**

Findings supported by credible evidence subject to documented limitations.

**Uncertain or disputed findings**

Findings involving material uncertainty or credible disagreement.

**Unknown / insufficient evidence**

Questions for which available evidence does not support a conclusion.

Also identify the five highest-priority follow-up research tasks.

## 5. Final Research Instructions

Conduct a thorough investigation rather than providing a high-level overview.

Prioritize research depth, source quality, citation accuracy, and transparency over brevity.

Do not stop at the first credible source when additional authoritative evidence could materially improve the finding.

Search historical sources where necessary to reconstruct the provision's origin and development.

Use secondary sources to discover additional primary evidence.

Do not treat the absence of readily available data as proof that no data exist.

Distinguish unsuccessful research from demonstrated nonexistence of evidence.

Do not invent statutory interpretations, legislative rationales, fiscal estimates, empirical findings, or source citations.

When evidence conflicts, explain the conflict.

When evidence is insufficient, state that explicitly.

Do not force a conclusion simply because the research template contains a field for it.

**The objective is not to produce the most confident-looking report. It is to produce the most accurate, comprehensive, transparent, and independently verifiable research possible.**

The final report should be suitable for subsequent human and AI-assisted source verification, comparison with an independently generated research report, and conversion into a publicly accessible Provision Project research record.

Begin the research.
