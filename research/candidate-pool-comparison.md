# Initial candidate-pool comparison

> Recovered research artifact for [Issue #6](https://github.com/mattyplo/provision-project/issues/6). This document compares the controlling candidate pool as a methodology/template stress test. It does **not** select the final five provisions.

## Research provenance

- **Model:** GPT-5.6 Sol
- **Mode:** research recovery / source-backed reconnaissance
- **Date:** 2026-09-25
- **Controlling candidate source:** Issue #6 and its five candidate-pool comments
- **Project constraints:** `docs/methodology.md`, `docs/sources.md`, `docs/vision.md`, and `research/provision-template.md`

This recovery preserves the central distinctions required by the project: statutory eligibility is not observed use; observed use is not direct tax benefit; direct tax benefit is not economic incidence; and fiscal estimates of different types should not be treated as interchangeable.

The candidate comments are treated as the controlling pool. The former Mine Rescue Team Training Credit (§ 45N) remains excluded because the wild-card reconnaissance found that its operative credit terminated for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2021.

## Candidate research notes

### Agriculture

**Farm income averaging (§ 1301).** A relatively bounded record with an unusual mechanism. It is useful precisely because the legal mechanics can be explained without first reconstructing a large administrative program. The hard questions are empirical: who elects it, how large is the direct benefit, and whether credible evidence permits a broader incidence claim. The record should not infer a volatility rationale until contemporaneous legislative evidence establishes it.

**Soil and water conservation expenditures (§ 175).** This candidate tests the project's ability to describe a deduction against the correct capitalization baseline. Reported qualifying expenditures would not equal tax savings. A complete record would need to identify the expenditures covered, statutory percentage limits, recapture or disposition interactions where material, and the historical rationale for allowing current deduction.

**Crop-insurance proceeds deferral (§ 451(f)).** This is the cleanest timing case in the agriculture pool. Its research value comes from making the project quantify or describe a deferral without accidentally treating deferred income as excluded income. Provision-specific incidence evidence may be limited; if so, the appropriate conclusion is **Economic incidence: Unknown / insufficient evidence**.

**Conservation cost-sharing exclusion (§ 126).** This is a boundary case because the tax result depends on qualifying programs and statutory conditions that reach beyond a simple “farmer receives payment” description. Research must identify which programs qualify under current law and which agencies administer them. It is a useful test of whether the source map can combine tax statute with program authorities without blurring their roles.

### Lower-income households / workers

**EITC (§ 32).** The pool's richest empirical benchmark. It can exercise nearly every part of the template: current-law eligibility, observed take-up, improper-payment/administration evidence, distribution, fiscal estimates, labor-supply research, and incidence. Its abundance of evidence is itself a test: the record must synthesize without treating findings from one historical EITC design or population as universally current.

**CTC/ACTC (§ 24).** The central challenge is version control. Credit amount, refundability, phase-in/phaseout rules and temporary expansions have changed repeatedly. A record needs explicit effective dates and should attach every distributional or empirical claim to the law version and tax years actually studied.

**Premium Tax Credit (§ 36B).** This provision tests cross-program administration better than almost any other household candidate. Advance premium tax credits affect cash flow before return filing and are reconciled later. The record must separate the statutory tax credit from Marketplace administration and avoid assuming that the household's statutory credit equals the household's ultimate economic gain.

**Saver's Credit / Saver's Match (§ 25B).** The candidate is unusually useful because the same Code section is undergoing a major redesign. The project must decide whether one record can coherently encompass the legacy credit and replacement mechanism, or whether the provision boundary needs explicit temporal subcomponents. Empirical evidence for the old regime cannot automatically establish effects of the new one.

### Large corporations / industry-specific

**Research Credit (§ 41).** This is a high-evidence business case with complex definitions and extensive empirical work. It tests whether the project can keep three separate questions apart: who claims the credit, whether it changes R&D activity, and who ultimately captures economic value.

**Bonus depreciation (§ 168(k)).** The strongest candidate for testing fiscal-timing language. Budget-window revenue estimates can show large near-term losses and later offsets because deductions move across years. The record must not translate a timing estimate into a permanent subsidy without an appropriate measurement basis.

**Intangible drilling and development costs (§ 263(c)).** This provides an old, industry-specific cost-recovery rule and a historical contrast with bonus depreciation. A rigorous record should define the capitalization counterfactual and avoid treating the amount deducted as tax savings. Economic incidence may extend beyond the firms legally making the election and should be established empirically, not inferred.

**LIHTC (§ 42).** The most structurally complex incidence case in the pool. Federal allocation, state agencies, project owners, investors, syndicators, developers, land markets and tenants can all matter. It strongly exercises the template's legal-claimant/direct-benefit/incidence distinctions and may expose a need for explicit representation of intermediary chains.

### Educators / teachers

**Educator-expense deduction regime (§§ 62/67/162).** The key question is the unit of analysis. The candidate already demonstrates that a meaningful “provision” may span multiple sections. It is a useful early test of the template's Boundaries and Related provisions fields.

**Teacher Loan Forgiveness exclusion (§ 108(f)(1) plus education law).** This candidate should not be researched as though § 108 creates teacher loan forgiveness. The underlying forgiveness authority and the federal income-tax consequence are separate legal layers. The first full-research task should be to verify exactly which teacher-specific forgiveness circumstances are covered under current § 108.

**Qualified tuition reductions (§ 117(d)).** This is an employment-benefit exclusion with educator/institution-specific boundaries and special graduate teaching/research treatment. The likely methodological challenge is data scarcity: legal eligibility may be clear while observed use, direct-benefit distribution and incidence are not.

**Qualified campus lodging (§ 119(d)).** An intentionally obscure candidate. It is valuable if the MVP should test what happens when official fiscal and observed-use datasets do not isolate a narrow rule. A complete record may legitimately contain multiple Unknown evidence states rather than filling gaps with inference.

### Wild cards

**Alaska Native subsistence whaling expenses (§ 170(n)).** Extreme specificity makes this a strong provenance test. The statutory connection to recognition by the Alaska Eskimo Whaling Commission must be represented accurately, while the rationale for charitable-contribution treatment should come from enactment-era sources rather than intuition. Fiscal/use evidence may be too sparse for a confident incidence conclusion.

**Railroad Track Maintenance Credit (§ 45G).** This combines a narrow industry rule with unusually useful legislative history: temporary enactment, repeated extension and later permanence/redesign. Assignment of eligible track miles makes the legal claimant less obvious than a simple railroad-credit label suggests. It is a strong “tax extender” history case.

**Federal Distilled Spirits Excise Tax (§ 5001 et seq.).** This candidate changes the ontology of the MVP because it is a tax regime rather than a tax expenditure or preferential treatment. The historical record must distinguish the 1791 whiskey excise as ancestry from the continuous federal taxation of distilled spirits beginning in 1862 and from continuity of today's exact Code provisions. Fiscal treatment must likewise distinguish excise receipts/revenue estimates from tax-expenditure estimates used elsewhere.

## Cross-pool analysis

### What the pool represents well

The pool already has strong mechanism diversity: refundable and nonrefundable credits, deductions, exclusions, income averaging, deferral, accelerated cost recovery, special valuation, and excise taxation. It also spans households, individual workers, pass-through/business taxpayers, corporations, narrow industries, occupations, educational institutions, and program-linked recipients.

Legislative-history diversity is unusually strong. At one end are modern provisions with discrete enactments; at the other are old industry rules and the distilled-spirits regime, where ancestry and statutory continuity become separate research questions. The railroad credit adds the repeated-extender pattern, while § 25B adds a live redesign problem.

The pool also contains multiple strong tests of the project's core warning that claimant identity is not economic incidence. LIHTC, the PTC, the Research Credit, bonus depreciation, § 45G and industry-specific cost recovery all potentially involve behavioral or market effects beyond the legal claimant. These are not reasons to assert a different beneficiary; they are reasons to demand evidence.

### Evidence-density variation

EITC, CTC, PTC, Research Credit, bonus depreciation and LIHTC are likely to have comparatively rich official and empirical literatures. Qualified campus lodging, whaling expenses, narrow agriculture elections and some educator benefits are likely to have much thinner provision-specific observed-use and incidence evidence.

That contrast is valuable. An MVP containing only evidence-rich provisions would not test whether the template handles legitimate Unknown states gracefully. Conversely, too many sparse cases could make it hard to test the project's richer incidence and distribution fields.

### Cross-program and cross-agency dependencies

The strongest cases are § 126 (conservation programs), § 36B (Marketplace/health-insurance administration), teacher loan forgiveness (§ 108 plus education law), LIHTC (§ 42 plus state allocating agencies), § 170(n) (Alaska Eskimo Whaling Commission recognition), and distilled-spirits excise administration (including TTB). These candidates can expose whether the source map adequately records non-tax authorities that are necessary to explain a tax result.

### Provision-definition boundary cases

The educator-expense regime, teacher-loan-forgiveness exclusion, Saver's Credit/Match transition, LIHTC, and § 5001 et seq. all challenge a one-section/one-provision assumption in different ways. This is one of the clearest reasons to keep “Boundaries” prominent in the template.

### Blind spots

The pool is intentionally shaped by the five discovery categories, so it is not a representative sample of the Code. It has little direct coverage of estate/gift taxation, international taxation, tax-exempt organizations as taxpayers, payroll taxes, municipal bonds, capital-gains preferences, or purely procedural provisions. That is not necessarily a defect for the first five; it is a limit on what the MVP can claim to have stress-tested.

## Methodology and template stress test

The existing methodology and template already anticipate many problems this pool exposes, especially explicit boundaries, multiple historical roots, evidence states, separation of observed use/direct benefit/incidence, fiscal-estimate metadata, source roles, and project inferences.

The candidate pool nevertheless identifies several questions to watch during the first records:

1. **Temporal provision identity.** § 25B and repeatedly redesigned credits raise the question of when a major redesign remains the same provision record versus becoming a successor record.
2. **Interacting-section identity.** The educator-expense regime shows why a record cannot always be keyed conceptually to one Code section.
3. **Non-Title-26 dependencies.** Teacher loan forgiveness, PTC administration, conservation programs and other cases require a clear way to distinguish the tax rule from the external program that supplies eligibility facts or benefits.
4. **Timing-value presentation.** § 451(f), § 168(k), § 175 and § 263(c) require disciplined counterfactuals and careful distinction between deduction/deferral amounts and actual tax value.
5. **Incidence with intermediary chains.** LIHTC in particular may need more structured representation of multiple parties and transmission channels than a single prose assessment.
6. **Sparse-data completion.** Campus lodging and whaling expenses test whether “initially complete” can comfortably coexist with Unknown observed-use, distribution, fiscal or incidence fields.
7. **Tax versus tax expenditure.** The distilled-spirits excise candidate tests whether the template's “fiscal effect” language works equally well for a revenue-raising tax regime and for preferential treatments.
8. **Historical ancestry versus continuity.** The distilled-spirits case makes explicit a distinction that can matter elsewhere: first appearance of a policy idea, continuous operation of a tax, and continuous identity of the exact modern statutory rule are different claims.

These are observations for the first-five research process, not proposed template changes. Actual revisions should follow evidence from completed provision records.

## Plausible five-provision portfolios

These are deliberately alternatives, not rankings or a final selection.

### Portfolio A — maximum mechanism and evidence contrast

- Agriculture: **Crop-insurance proceeds deferral (§ 451(f))**
- Lower-income: **EITC (§ 32)**
- Corporate/industry: **LIHTC (§ 42)**
- Educators: **Qualified campus lodging (§ 119(d))**
- Wild card: **Federal Distilled Spirits Excise Tax (§ 5001 et seq.)**

This portfolio combines a narrow timing election, an evidence-rich refundable credit, a multi-party financing credit, a sparse-data employment benefit, and a historically deep excise regime. It strongly tests incidence, sparse evidence, cross-agency administration, and historical lineage. It does less to test accelerated depreciation or a live statutory redesign.

### Portfolio B — provision-boundary and cross-program stress

- Agriculture: **Conservation cost-sharing exclusion (§ 126)**
- Lower-income: **Premium Tax Credit (§ 36B)**
- Corporate/industry: **Bonus depreciation (§ 168(k))**
- Educators: **Teacher Loan Forgiveness exclusion (§ 108(f)(1) plus education law)**
- Wild card: **Railroad Track Maintenance Credit (§ 45G)**

This portfolio emphasizes external-program dependencies, reconciliation/administration, timing economics, non-Title-26 law, and extender history. It would force early decisions about provision boundaries. It has less of the extreme evidence-scarcity test and lacks the very long historical lineage of distilled spirits.

### Portfolio C — historical development and redesign

- Agriculture: **Farm income averaging (§ 1301)**
- Lower-income: **Saver's Credit / Saver's Match (§ 25B)**
- Corporate/industry: **Intangible drilling and development costs (§ 263(c))**
- Educators: **Educator-expense deduction regime (§§ 62/67/162)**
- Wild card: **Federal Distilled Spirits Excise Tax (§ 5001 et seq.)**

This portfolio foregrounds lineage, redesign, old industry-specific treatment, interacting Code sections, and codification history. It is particularly useful for testing how the project narrates change over time. It gives up some of the unusually rich modern incidence literature available for EITC, LIHTC and the Research Credit.

### Portfolio D — empirical/incidence-heavy

- Agriculture: **Soil and water conservation expenditures (§ 175)**
- Lower-income: **EITC (§ 32)**
- Corporate/industry: **Research Credit (§ 41)**
- Educators: **Qualified tuition reductions (§ 117(d))**
- Wild card: **Railroad Track Maintenance Credit (§ 45G)**

This portfolio pairs two evidence-rich major credits with narrower deduction/exclusion/industry cases. It should produce substantial work on behavioral evidence and claimant-versus-incidence distinctions while still testing sparse observed-use data. It does less to test cross-program administration than Portfolio B and less to test very deep statutory ancestry than portfolios containing distilled spirits.

## Research ambiguities to resolve before final selection

The comparison should not be treated as final reconnaissance on current law. Before selecting the five, deeper source work should resolve at least these candidate-specific questions:

- the exact current-law transition and implementation status of the Saver's Match under § 25B;
- the precise interaction between § 108 and teacher-specific loan-forgiveness authorities;
- whether available official datasets isolate use or fiscal effects for the narrow educator, agriculture and whaling provisions;
- the appropriate unit of analysis for § 5001 et seq. and whether the first record should cover the core excise or the broader distilled-spirits regime;
- comparable fiscal measures for each candidate without collapsing tax expenditures, revenue estimates, timing effects, credit amounts, deductions/exclusions reported, and observed receipts into a single “cost” number.
