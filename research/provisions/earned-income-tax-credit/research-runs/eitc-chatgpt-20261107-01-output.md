# Federal Earned Income Tax Credit — 26 U.S.C. § 32

## Executive synthesis and provision boundary

This report applies the Provision Project’s EITC research assignment, version “EITC 1.0,” supplied for this run. fileciteturn0file0 The legal snapshot is **October 31, 2026**. The primary statutory unit is 26 U.S.C. §32, but a coherent account also requires limited reference to §152 for the qualifying-child rules; §6013 and §7703 for marital and nonresident-alien rules; §6402(m) for refund timing; §6695(g) and Treas. Reg. §1.6695-2 for paid-preparer due diligence; and administrative rules governing refundability, Social Security numbers, and filing. State EITCs, the Child Tax Credit, SNAP, TANF, the minimum wage, and antipoverty policy generally are outside the provision boundary except where they illuminate administration, interactions, or empirical identification. citeturn201507search2turn782507search8turn782507search12

**Core finding — [EITC-C001, Established].** Section 32 is a refundable earnings-conditioned individual income-tax credit. Its statutory formula rises with earned income at a specified credit percentage, reaches a maximum, and later declines at a specified phaseout percentage once the greater of adjusted gross income or earned income exceeds a threshold. The percentage and dollar schedule varies with the number of qualifying children, while the statute imposes separate eligibility, investment-income, identification-number, filing-status, and other conditions. citeturn711151view0turn201507search2

The **direct statutory beneficiary** is the eligible tax filer or filing unit to whom the credit is allowed. That is not necessarily the same as the ultimate economic beneficiary: wage responses, labor-supply changes, employer capture, and within-household effects can shift part of the economic value. The empirical literature strongly justifies separating those questions. citeturn478749search1turn478749search3

The evidence is strongest on current statutory mechanics and enactment history, reasonably strong but measurement-dependent on observed claims and take-up, and materially less settled on ultimate incidence. In particular, conventional studies report sizable employment responses among some single mothers, while a major later reappraisal argues that most EITC expansions do not generate robust employment effects once the unusually confounded 1993 episode is separated from other reforms. citeturn478749search0turn512832search0turn512832search2

The October 31, 2026 snapshot also contains a significant administrative-law wrinkle. In August 2026 Treasury and IRS **proposed**, but had not yet finalized, regulations that would treat the refunded portion of certain refundable income-tax credits, including the EITC, as a “Federal public benefit” under PRWORA. A scheduled October 14 hearing was cancelled because no requests to testify were received. IRS filing materials were already preparing for Schedule 3-A treatment for tax year 2026, but the proposed regulation stated that its substantive rules would apply only once final regulations were published. Accordingly, the proposed PRWORA rule should not be treated as operative substantive §32 law as of October 31, 2026. [EITC-C014, Supported.] citeturn711151view11turn711151view12turn549904view2

The House Code version inspected is current through Public Law 119-124, enacted October 15, 2026; its amendment history identifies the most recent direct amendment of §32 as the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021. The research found no later direct §32 amendment through the October 31 snapshot. That negative finding is **Supported**, not “Established,” because the codifier version itself stops at October 15 and the conclusion also depends on targeted searches for intervening legislation. citeturn711151view0turn201507search2

**Research-completion assessment.** Established findings include the statutory formula, principal 2026 eligibility rules, inflation-adjusted 2026 parameters, the 1975 statutory creation, and JCT’s published fiscal estimates. Supported findings include the principal documented rationales for enactment, recent participation estimates, and several behavioral effects subject to population and identification limits. The magnitude of labor-supply effects and employer capture is **disputed or uncertain**. A single nationwide estimate of ultimate economic incidence is **Unknown / insufficiently established**.

## Current law and tax-year applicability

For tax year 2026, the statutory structure is best understood as three regions. In the **phase-in**, the credit equals the applicable credit percentage multiplied by earned income, until the inflation-adjusted earned-income amount is reached. The credit then sits on a **maximum-credit plateau**. Once the greater of AGI or earned income exceeds the applicable phaseout threshold, the credit declines at the statutory phaseout rate until it reaches zero. The IRS’s Revenue Procedure 2025-32 supplies the inflation-adjusted 2026 amounts; those are **tax-year 2026 parameters**, not 2026 filing-season figures for tax year 2025. A taxpayer without a qualifying child generally must have a principal U.S. abode for more than half the tax year, must be at least 25 but under 65 at year-end, and cannot be another taxpayer’s dependent. A taxpayer with a qualifying child is governed instead by the qualifying-child framework incorporating §152(c), with §32-specific modifications. Section 32 also excludes a claimant who claims §911 foreign-earned-income benefits and generally excludes a nonresident alien for any part of the year unless the applicable §6013 election treats the individual as a U.S. resident. [EITC-C003, Established.] citeturn201507search0turn201507search2

“Earned income” principally includes taxable wages, salaries, tips, other employee compensation and net earnings from self-employment, subject to statutory adjustments and exclusions. Section 32 separately excludes certain pension or annuity amounts, certain compensation earned while an inmate, and other specified income; combat pay receives special elective treatment. citeturn711151view0turn201507search2

The investment-income rule is a complete eligibility bar rather than an additional phaseout. ARPA permanently raised the statutory base from $2,200 to $10,000 and subjected the amount to inflation adjustment beginning from the new statutory base. For tax year 2026, the IRS-adjusted ceiling is **$12,200**. Disqualified income includes specified taxable and tax-exempt interest, dividends, net rents and royalties, capital-gain net income, and certain passive income. citeturn711151view0turn711151view1

For identification, §32 requires taxpayer identification numbers and, for a qualifying child to be taken into account, the required identifying number. The present statute defines the relevant number as a Social Security number issued by the Social Security Administration on or before the return’s due date, including extensions, under the applicable statutory conditions. citeturn487885view1turn201507search2

For married claimants, a joint return is ordinarily required, but ARPA added a permanent separated-spouse rule: an individual who does not file jointly can be treated as unmarried for EITC purposes when the taxpayer resides with a qualifying child for more than half the year and either did not share an abode with the spouse during the last six months or meets the specified separation-instrument and separate-household conditions. The amendment generally applies to tax years beginning after December 31, 2020. citeturn201507search1turn717624view1

## Origin, rationale, and development history

**Original enactment — [EITC-C004, Established].** Congress created the recognizable federal earned-income credit in §204 of the **Tax Reduction Act of 1975**, Pub. L. 94-12, H.R. 2166, enacted March 29, 1975. The provision inserted new §43 into the 1954 Code. It allowed 10 percent of the first $4,000 of earned income, producing a maximum $400 credit, and reduced the credit by 10 percent of income above $4,000, so the credit disappeared at $8,000. Eligibility was tied to maintaining a U.S. household that included a qualifying dependent child under the then-applicable rules. The original provision was temporary. citeturn711151view2turn670163view0turn549904view0

The original credit was refundable. That design mattered because families with little or no income-tax liability could receive value beyond simply reducing positive income-tax liability; refundability has therefore been part of the provision’s architecture from its origin rather than a later add-on. citeturn506934search8turn711151view2

**Why it was created — [EITC-C005, Supported].** The contemporaneous policy story is plural rather than singular. CRS’s reconstruction of the 1975 legislative history identifies several overlapping purposes: offsetting the burden of Social Security/payroll taxes on low-income workers, strengthening work incentives relative to cash welfare or negative-income-tax alternatives, providing income support to working families, and supplying short-term tax relief during the 1974–75 economic downturn. CRS traces the work-incentive lineage in part to Senator Russell Long’s “work bonus” proposals, developed in the context of debates over President Nixon’s Family Assistance Plan. These rationales are well supported historically, but they should not be collapsed into a single “intent of Congress.” citeturn506934search3turn506934search8

The Senate Finance Committee report cited by the retrospective CRS history, S. Rep. 94-36, is an especially important contemporaneous source for payroll-tax relief and work-incentive rationales. In this run it was **identified through the CRS source trail but not independently retrieved and inspected**, so propositions attributed specifically to that report remain “Identified but not verified” pending source audit. citeturn506934search3

## Observed use, direct benefit, administration, and compliance

Observed use must be kept separate from legal eligibility. IRS Statistics of Income material reports approximately **23 million tax-year-2023 returns with EITC totaling about $64 billion**, compared with approximately 23 million returns and $57 billion for tax year 2022. Tax year 2021 had roughly 31 million EITC returns and $64 billion of credit, reflecting, among other factors, the temporary ARPA expansion for workers without qualifying children. IRS warns that these tabulations generally reflect returns as processed after math-error correction and can differ from later amended-return or examination outcomes. [EITC-C007, Supported.] citeturn711151view3turn399284search1

Those figures are not the same thing as “taxpayers legally eligible,” “credits finally retained,” “refunds actually disbursed,” or “federal tax expenditure.” A return-level tax statistic is an administrative measure of claimed/processed use; eligibility requires a separate model or audit; final retained benefits can change through examinations and amendments; and fiscal-accounting estimates use different time conventions and baselines. citeturn711151view3turn670163view1

For **tax year 2022**, the IRS/Census linked-data participation model estimates a national participation rate of **80.8%**, meaning approximately 19.2% of modeled eligible tax units did not receive an EITC payment. The denominator is model-based eligibility derived from American Community Survey income and demographic data, linked to IRS records for actual payments. A separate Current Population Survey-based national estimate is 78%. The difference illustrates why “take-up” is not a directly observed count of all eligible households. [EITC-C008, Supported.] citeturn711151view4

A separate IRS benefits-gap analysis of tax-year-2022 **filers** estimated roughly 452,000 nonclaiming filers with about $848 million in unclaimed EITC and about 17,000 underclaimants with roughly $45 million additionally available—approximately $893 million across about 470,000 filer tax units. That study excludes eligible nonfilers and uses an administrative/modeling framework different from the participation-rate series, so it should not be used as a substitute for national take-up. citeturn399284search5turn399284search6

**Direct tax benefit.** For a claimant who satisfies §32, the immediate legal benefit is the credit allowed against federal income tax. To the extent the allowable refundable credit exceeds tax otherwise due, the excess can be treated as an overpayment and paid as a refund under the applicable refund provisions. Thus the statutory recipient is the taxpayer or joint-return filing unit, while the fiscal presentation separates the portion reducing receipts from the refundable outlay component. [EITC-C001, Established.] citeturn201507search2turn711151view11

Refundability does not mean every dollar appearing on an EITC return is necessarily delivered as a separate check. Some amount can offset income-tax liability; refundable amounts can interact with refund-offset procedures; refunds claiming the EITC are subject to §6402(m)’s statutory hold until mid-February; and later examinations or amended returns can alter entitlement. citeturn782507search12turn711151view3

**Administration.** The IRS implements the credit through Form 1040 and related schedules, EITC tables and worksheets, SSN validation, return processing, math-error procedures, examinations, and post-disallowance rules. Paid return preparers who determine EITC eligibility or amount are subject to §6695(g) and Treas. Reg. §1.6695-2 due-diligence requirements, including completion and submission or retention of Form 8867 as applicable. citeturn782507search0turn782507search4turn782507search8

The principal external dependencies are narrow but important. The Social Security Administration supplies the SSNs on which the statutory identification rule depends; §152 supplies much of the qualifying-child framework; Census microdata are used for IRS/Census take-up estimates; and PRWORA is the external legal regime implicated by the 2026 proposed refundable-credit regulation. None of those dependencies makes the entire external program part of the §32 research boundary. citeturn487885view1turn711151view4turn711151view11

**Improper payments.** GAO reported an EITC improper-payment rate of **27.3% for fiscal year 2024**, down from 33.5% for fiscal year 2023. The government’s improper-payment concept includes payments that should not have been made, payments in incorrect amounts, or payments to ineligible recipients; it is not synonymous with proven intentional fraud. The causes cited in government oversight include taxpayer misreporting, eligibility complexity, verification constraints and program-design limitations. [EITC-C009, Supported.] citeturn711151view5turn399284search7

That rate must not be converted into “27.3% of EITC taxpayers committed fraud.” It is an estimated payment-integrity measure with its own sampling and methodology, expressed for a fiscal-year reporting context. Fraud requires a different evidentiary showing, while EITC error can include relationship or residency mistakes, incorrect income, missing substantiation, or other nonfraudulent legal ineligibility. citeturn711151view5

## Economic incidence, distribution, and empirical disagreements

The labor-supply literature is the most consequential area of disagreement. Eissa and Liebman’s influential difference-in-differences study of the Tax Reform Act of 1986 compared single women with children—who were exposed to the EITC expansion—with women without children. It estimated an increase in labor-force participation on the order of 1.4 percentage points from a pre-reform participation base near 73%, but found little evidence of an hours response among those already working. The study’s design supports an **extensive-margin** causal interpretation under its identifying assumptions, but the 1986 tax reform changed provisions besides the EITC, creating an attribution limitation. [EITC-C011, Supported but bounded.] citeturn478749search0

A later synthesis by Schanzenbach and Strain, examining multiple federal EITC expansions, argues that the accumulated evidence supports positive extensive-margin employment effects. That conclusion is broadly consistent with the traditional literature emphasizing increased employment among single mothers. citeturn512832search2turn478749search3

Kleven’s 2024 *Journal of Public Economics* reappraisal reaches a materially more skeptical conclusion. Using event-study evidence around a broad set of federal and state EITC reforms, Kleven reports that, apart from the 1993 expansion, employment responses tend to cluster around zero; he argues that the unusually large 1993 change is difficult to separate from contemporaneous welfare reform and macroeconomic forces. This directly challenges treating the older “large employment effect” estimate as a universal feature of EITC expansions. [EITC-C011, Disputed.] citeturn512832search0

The disagreement is therefore not well summarized as “the EITC does” or “does not” increase employment. A more defensible statement is that some quasi-experimental studies identify positive participation effects for particular groups and reforms, especially single mothers, while newer reanalysis questions whether those effects generalize across expansions and historical settings. The intensive-margin evidence—hours conditional on employment—is generally weaker and often small. citeturn478749search0turn512832search0turn478749search3

**Employer and wage incidence — [EITC-C012, Uncertain].** Rothstein’s incidence analysis uses a labor-market simulation/calibration rather than a clean causal wage experiment. Under preferred elasticity assumptions, it concludes that employers can capture a meaningful share of the subsidy through lower equilibrium wages; one calibration implies workers’ after-tax income rises by roughly $0.73 per dollar of government expenditure. That is a **theoretical/simulated prediction**, not a nationwide observed incidence estimate, and it depends materially on labor-supply and labor-demand elasticities. citeturn478749search1

Other empirical work has reported suggestive wage patterns consistent with some employer capture, but estimates are often imprecise and identification is difficult because eligibility itself is correlated with family status and labor-market behavior. The evidence therefore supports employer capture as a credible channel, but does not establish a single national share of EITC value accruing to employers. citeturn478749search7turn478749search3

**Family outcomes.** Hoynes, Miller and Simon exploit tax-reform variation to study infant health. Their quasi-experimental analysis estimates that a $1,000 increase in EITC exposure was associated with a roughly 6.7–10.8% reduction in low birthweight among the studied population of low-education single mothers, with larger estimated effects for Black mothers; proposed mechanisms include prenatal care and maternal smoking. This is evidence about a downstream household outcome, not a direct measure of labor-market incidence or employer capture. [EITC-C013, Supported within population.] citeturn478749search2

These are modeled JCT distribution figures, not an IRS count of finally allowed tax-year-2025 claims and not a measure of ultimate economic incidence. citeturn670163view1

That distribution shows where the modeled **tax credit** is assigned under JCT’s income concept. Those incidence questions remain empirically separate. citeturn478749search1turn670163view1

**Economic-incidence conclusion:** **Uncertain / disputed.** The evidence supports direct statutory transfers to eligible filing units and establishes that labor supply and household outcomes can respond in some populations. It also supplies a plausible theoretical basis for employer capture. But the literature retrieved does not establish a single nationwide division of EITC economic value among workers, employers, spouses, children, or other parties under the current 2026 regime. A stronger conclusion would require contemporary causal evidence jointly measuring wages, employment, hours, household allocation and employer behavior across the present-law schedule.

## Fiscal effect and reconciliation

JCT’s **Estimates of Federal Tax Expenditures for Fiscal Years 2025–2029**, JCX-45-25, estimates the EITC tax expenditure at **$66.9 billion in FY2025, $67.2 billion in FY2026, $67.4 billion in FY2027, $67.6 billion in FY2028 and $67.8 billion in FY2029**, for $336.9 billion over the five-year window. JCT expressly identifies the EITC estimate as including the refundable/outlay effect. [EITC-C010, Established as an official estimate.] citeturn238081view0turn670163view1

The same JCT publication separately reports an EITC **outlay component** of $56.2 billion for FY2025, $56.6 billion for FY2026, $56.8 billion in each of FY2027–FY2029, totaling $283.2 billion. The difference between the total tax-expenditure estimate and that outlay component is approximately the receipt-reduction/non-outlay component, subject to rounding and JCT’s conventions. For FY2026, simple subtraction gives about **$10.6 billion**. That $10.6 billion is a **project arithmetic inference**, not a separately printed JCT estimate. citeturn670163view1

JCT also explains that its tax-expenditure calculations project **actual claims** rather than the full theoretical benefits to which all legally eligible taxpayers might be entitled. That convention is important for a provision with incomplete take-up and erroneous claims in both directions. citeturn670163view1

Treasury’s tax-expenditure methodology likewise cautions that a tax-expenditure estimate is not mechanically the revenue that repeal would raise. Revenue estimates for legislation incorporate behavioral responses, interactions, implementation and a specified baseline in ways that a tax-expenditure table generally does not. citeturn319624search1

The 2025 reconciliation law can also affect refundable-credit budget accounting indirectly even without directly amending §32. CBO’s 2026 budget analysis explains that changes in underlying individual income-tax liability can change what portion of refundable credits appears as outlays. Consequently, a change in projected EITC outlays need not imply a change in the §32 credit formula itself. citeturn319624search2

## Research completion summary and draft research-run manifest

**Established findings.** The EITC was created by Pub. L. 94-12 in 1975; §32 now provides the earnings-conditioned phase-in/maximum/phaseout structure described above; 2026 inflation-adjusted amounts are set out in Rev. Proc. 2025-32; the current statute contains the described child, no-child, investment-income, marital-status and identification provisions; the 2021 childless expansion has expired; and JCT estimates the FY2026 EITC tax expenditure at $67.2 billion, including a $56.6 billion outlay component. citeturn549904view0turn711151view1turn201507search2turn670163view1

**Supported findings.** Contemporaneous and retrospective government evidence supports payroll-tax relief, work incentives and income support as important rationales for the original credit, though not as an exclusive unified congressional purpose. IRS data support approximately 23 million tax-year-2023 EITC returns and about $64 billion of credit; IRS/Census modeling supports a tax-year-2022 participation estimate of 80.8%; and empirical work supports some positive employment and family-outcome effects in specified populations. citeturn506934search3turn711151view3turn711151view4turn478749search0turn478749search2

**Uncertain or disputed findings.** The magnitude and generality of EITC employment responses are disputed, with traditional quasi-experimental findings challenged by later event-study reanalysis. Employer capture is theoretically plausible and supported by some suggestive evidence, but its nationwide magnitude is uncertain. The precise legal/administrative consequences of the 2026 PRWORA initiative were in transition at the snapshot date because the substantive regulation remained proposed. citeturn512832search0turn478749search1turn711151view11

**Unknown / insufficient evidence.** The research does not establish a single national estimate of ultimate EITC economic incidence under the 2026 regime; it does not provide a fully reconciled official series from filed claim through final retained payment for one common tax year; and it does not establish from directly inspected contemporaneous committee reports every rationale associated with the 1975 enactment. Those remain source-audit and follow-up issues.
