## 1. Research Assignment

You are conducting comprehensive legal, historical, fiscal, and economic research for the Provision Project, an open-source project documenting provisions of the United States federal tax code.

The project's central questions are:

1. What does this provision do?
2. Who legally qualifies for it?
3. When and how did it enter federal tax law?
4. Why was it created?
5. How has it changed over time?
6. Who actually uses it?
7. Who receives the direct tax benefit?
8. Who ultimately benefits economically, and what evidence supports that conclusion?
9. What is its estimated fiscal effect?
10. How confident can we be in each answer?

The objective is to produce a comprehensive, source-backed research report that can support the creation of a publicly accessible provision record.

This is an evidence-gathering exercise, not an argument for or against the provision.

Do not characterize the provision as a loophole, subsidy, giveaway, abuse, or otherwise make normative judgments unless describing and attributing a specific source's characterization.

The project does not require a definitive conclusion where evidence is insufficient.

An explicit finding of **Unknown** is preferable to an unsupported conclusion.

### Provision to research

- **Working name:** Federal Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC)
- **Primary Code citation:** 26 U.S.C. §32
- **Jurisdiction:** United States federal tax law
- **Current-law as-of date:** October 31, 2026
- **Historical coverage:** From the earliest identifiable statutory origin or recognizable predecessor through the current-law as-of date
- **Research scope:** The federal EITC under §32, including its predecessors, refundability, earned-income and investment-income rules, qualifying-child and childless-worker rules, phase-in/plateau/phase-out calculations, filing and administration, observed claims and take-up, fiscal estimates, behavioral effects, and economic incidence. Examine related Code provisions only as needed to explain §32; state EITCs and other transfer programs are contextual comparators, not independent research subjects.

### EITC-specific research requirements

- Trace the **original enactment in the 1970s** and investigate contemporaneous rationales (including payroll-tax relief, work incentives, and income support) without presuming any one rationale is definitive. Verify statutory origin and later expansions against primary sources.
- Provide an intelligible, dated explanation of the **phase-in, maximum-credit plateau, and phase-out**; distinguish tax year 2026 parameters from earlier years and from the publication dates of IRS data.
- Investigate **2025–2026 enacted changes** where relevant. Distinguish enacted, operative, future-effective, and transitional rules using statutory effective-date provisions, rather than relying on undated summaries.
- Build a **tax-year applicability matrix** for material EITC rules as of the October 31, 2026 snapshot. For each rule, identify the specific statutory/administrative authority, enactment and effective dates, operative tax year(s), transition or expiration, and whether it is operative, enacted-but-future-effective, or historical as of the snapshot. Include the relevant annual inflation-adjusted thresholds and their dated IRS authority; do not infer tax-year parameters from the filing season or data publication date.
- Examine qualifying children, taxpayers without qualifying children, earned and investment income, filing status, residency, identification rules, refundability, disallowance, and compliance administration. Verify the governing Code/regulatory provisions.
- For observed use, separate **claims filed, claims allowed, number of taxpayers, credit dollars, refunds/outlays, take-up estimates, and improper-payment estimates**. Identify tax year versus fiscal year, denominators, revisions, and measurement uncertainty.
- For fiscal effects, search JCT and Treasury tax expenditure publications, CBO and federal budget materials; distinguish estimates of revenue loss, outlays, and the revenue effect of hypothetical policy changes.
- Evaluate labor supply (extensive and intensive margins), wages, employer incidence, family outcomes, and distribution using empirical literature; identify study populations, periods, identification strategies, and disagreements. Do not equate a causal estimate from one setting with a universal effect.
- Include state EITCs and interactions with other programs only where necessary to interpret the federal EITC, clearly outside the main provision boundary.

### Provision boundaries

The unit of analysis is the substantive tax provision, not necessarily the entire Code section or every program related to it.

Begin by investigating and establishing the precise boundaries of the provision.

Identify:

- The tax treatment being studied.
- The transactions, payments, activities, and taxpayers covered.
- Related Code sections necessary to understand its operation.
- Related provisions that should remain outside the research boundary.
- External government programs that materially affect eligibility, administration, or implementation.

Do not assume the preliminary working scope is legally complete or accurate.

Where the provision's boundaries are ambiguous, explain the ambiguity and recommend a defensible research boundary.

Do not silently expand the study to encompass state EITCs, the Child Tax Credit, SNAP, TANF, minimum-wage policy, or antipoverty policy generally. Discuss interactions only where material to federal §32.

## 2. Research Standards

### Source hierarchy

Prioritize primary and nonpartisan official sources.

Preferred sources include:

**Statutory and legislative sources**
- United States Code
- Public Laws and Statutes at Large

**Administrative and government sources**
- Treasury regulations

- Official government administrative data
- Relevant federal agency documentation

**Economic and historical research**
- Peer-reviewed academic research
- National Bureau of Economic Research working papers
- Credible university and research-institution publications
- Other methodologically transparent empirical research

**Supplementary sources**
- Professional tax commentary
- Industry publications
- Historical accounts
- Journalism
- Stakeholder and advocacy materials

Secondary sources may help identify primary documents, explain technical concepts, or present empirical findings.

However, do not rely on secondary summaries when the relevant primary source can reasonably be obtained.

### Source provenance

For consequential claims, provide:

- Source title
- Author or issuing institution
- Publication date
- Document identifier, where applicable
- Stable URL or document locator
- Relevant page, section, table, paragraph, or statutory subsection
- Whether the source is primary or secondary
- Whether the source is contemporaneous or retrospective, when relevant
- Whether the source directly supports the claim or provides contextual evidence

Do not invent citations, quotations, page numbers, URLs, document identifiers, or statistical estimates.

Do not present a source as directly inspected unless its contents were actually accessed.

If a source is identified but cannot be retrieved, label it **Identified but not verified**.

If a source is inaccessible, explain the limitation.

### Citation accuracy

A citation must support the specific claim to which it is attached.

Distinguish:

- **Direct support:** The source directly supports the claim.
- **Contextual support:** The source provides relevant context but is insufficient by itself.
- **Lead only:** The source identifies potentially relevant evidence requiring independent verification.
- **Inference support:** The source supplies evidence used in an explicitly identified analytical inference.

Do not treat an AI-generated summary as independent evidence of the underlying claim.

### Evidence states

Use the following evidence states when assessing findings:

| State | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Established | Direct, authoritative evidence establishes the finding with no material unresolved qualification |
| Supported | Credible evidence supports the finding, subject to stated limitations |
| Uncertain | Evidence permits a tentative assessment but important uncertainty remains |
| Disputed | Credible sources reach materially conflicting conclusions |
| Unknown | Available evidence does not support a conclusion |

These are working descriptions; apply the project's methodology when making final classifications.

Use **Unknown** when evidence is insufficient. Explain the gap rather than inventing a result.

Do not substitute a numerical confidence score for an evidence-based explanation.

## 3.1 Provision Definition and Scope

### Substantive Mechanism
Section 32 of the Internal Revenue Code (26 U.S.C. § 32) allows an "eligible individual" a refundable tax credit against federal income tax imposed by subtitle A equal to the "credit percentage" of so much of the taxpayer's "earned income" for the taxable year as does not exceed the "earned income amount," reduced (but not below zero) by the "phaseout percentage" of the amount by which the taxpayer's adjusted gross income (or, if greater, earned income) exceeds the "threshold phaseout amount" (§ 32(a)(1)–(2)).

Unlike nonrefundable personal credits governed by Subpart A of Part IV of Subchapter A of Chapter 1 (§§ 21–26), the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) is placed in Subpart C ("Refundable Credits," §§ 31–37). By operation of 26 U.S.C. § 6401(b)(1), if the amount allowable as a credit under Subpart C exceeds the tax imposed by Subtitle A (reduced by nonrefundable credits), the excess is considered an "overpayment" of tax, which the Secretary of the Treasury is authorized to refund or credit to the taxpayer under 26 U.S.C. § 6402(a).

### Primary Statutory Authority
- **26 U.S.C. § 32** (Earned income), subsections (a) through (n).
- **26 CFR § 1.32-2** (Earned income credit for taxable years beginning after December 31, 1978) and **26 CFR § 1.32-3** (Eligibility requirements after denial of the earned income credit).

### Related Statutory Provisions Necessary to Understand Operation
To operate as a functional tax mechanism, § 32 incorporates or depends directly upon several outside sections of Title 26 and Title 31:
1. **26 U.S.C. § 152(c), (e), and (f) (Qualifying Child Definition):** Section 32(c)(3)(A) defines a "qualifying child" by cross-referencing § 152(c) (relationship, principal place of abode for more than one-half of the taxable year, and age tests), while stripping out the § 152(c)(1)(D) support test and substituting EITC-specific identification rules.
2. **26 U.S.C. § 6401(b) and § 6402(a), (c)–(f) (Refundability and Offset Authority):** Establish the legal mechanism converting excess § 32 credits into refundable tax overpayments and subjecting those refunds to administrative offsets for past-due child support, federal agency non-tax debts, and state income tax obligations.
3. **26 U.S.C. § 6213(g)(2) (Mathematical or Clerical Error Summary Assessment Authority):** Grants the IRS authority to summarily disallow EITC claims without issuing a statutory notice of deficiency where a return omits a required valid taxpayer identification number (TIN), presents an age/TIN mismatch, or claims the credit during a statutory disallowance period under § 32(k).
4. **26 U.S.C. § 6695(g) (Paid Preparer Due Diligence Penalty):** Imposes a per-failure civil penalty (indexed for inflation under § 6695(h); $650 per failure for returns filed in 2026 under Rev. Proc. 2024-40 / $660 for returns filed in 2027 under Rev. Proc. 2025-32) on paid tax return preparers who fail to comply with Treasury due diligence requirements (Form 8867; 26 CFR § 1.6695-2) in determining eligibility for, or the amount of, the EITC.
5. **26 U.S.C. § 6402(m) (Minimum Delay on EITC/ACTC Refunds):** Enacted by the PATH Act of 2015, prohibits the Secretary from issuing any credit or refund before February 15 of the calendar year following the taxable year for any return claiming the EITC or the Additional Child Tax Credit (ACTC).
6. **26 U.S.C. § 6409 (Disregard of Refunds in Federal and Federally Assisted Programs):** Provides that any tax refund received by an individual under Title 26 (including EITC refunds) shall not be taken into account as income, and shall not be taken into account as resources for a period of 12 months from receipt, for purposes of determining eligibility or benefit levels under any federal program or any state or local program financed in whole or in part with federal funds.
7. **26 U.S.C. § 7703(b) (Certain Married Individuals Living Apart):** Governs when a married individual living apart from their spouse with a qualifying child is treated as unmarried for head-of-household filing status, alongside the broader EITC-specific separated-spouse rule in § 32(d)(2).
8. **31 U.S.C. § 1324 (Refund of Internal Revenue Collections):** Provides the permanent, indefinite Congressional appropriation funding the outlay portion of refundable § 32 credits.

### Precise Research Boundary and Exclusions
The unit of analysis for this report is **the federal Earned Income Tax Credit under 26 U.S.C. § 32**, together with its immediate statutory predecessor (former 26 U.S.C. § 43, 1975–1984), its Repealed Advance Payment mechanism (former 26 U.S.C. § 3507, 1979–2010), and the administrative and compliance rules in Title 26 that directly govern § 32 eligibility, calculation, refundability, and enforcement.

**Important Exclusions from the Research Boundary:**
- **Child Tax Credit (CTC) and Additional Child Tax Credit (ACTC) (26 U.S.C. § 24):** Although § 24 and § 32 share the § 152(c) qualifying child framework and are often filed and audited together, § 24 is a distinct tax provision with its own statutory history, age cutoff (under age 17), phase-in rules, and higher phase-out thresholds. It is referenced only where necessary to explain overlapping qualifying-child rules, refund delay rules (§ 6402(m)), or joint empirical estimates.
- **State, Local, and Territorial EITCs (including 26 U.S.C. § 7527A / ARPA § 9625 payments to U.S. territories):** Over 30 states, the District of Columbia, Puerto Rico, and Guam operate territorial or state earned income credits, often structured as a percentage of the federal § 32 credit. These remain outside the provision boundary and are discussed solely where state EITC variation is used as an econometric control or comparator in empirical studies of federal § 32.
- **Direct Transfer Programs (TANF, SNAP, Medicaid, SSI):** Analyzed only where statutory dependencies (§ 6409 disregard rules) or empirical interaction effects bear directly on understanding the federal EITC.

### External-Program Dependencies
The federal EITC has two direct statutory dependencies on external non-tax government programs:
1. **Social Security Administration (SSA) Enumeration and Work-Authorization Records:** Under § 32(c)(1)(E), § 32(c)(3)(D), and § 32(m), a valid TIN for EITC purposes must be a Social Security Number issued by the SSA to a U.S. citizen or to a noncitizen pursuant to § 205(c)(2)(B)(i)(I) (or that portion of clause (III) relating to employment authorization) of the Social Security Act (42 U.S.C. § 405(c)(2)(B)(i)). Thus, EITC legal eligibility depends directly on SSA administrative classification of work-authorized SSNs versus non-work SSNs or IRS Individual Taxpayer Identification Numbers (ITINs).
2. **State and Tribal Foster Care and Authorized Placement Agencies:** Under § 32(c)(3)(A) and § 152(f)(1)(C), an eligible foster child qualifies only if placed with the taxpayer by an authorized placement agency or by judgment, decree, or other order of any court of competent jurisdiction.

### Coherence of the Research Boundary and Unresolved Boundary Questions
Treating § 32 (plus its § 6401(b)/§ 6402 refund engine and § 6213/§ 6695 compliance apparatus) as a single provision boundary is legally and economically coherent because the phase-in, plateau, phase-out, and refundability rules operate as one unified formula on Schedule EIC and the Form 1040 EIC Worksheet.

One technical boundary question arises in **federal budget accounting**: the Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT), the Office of Management and Budget (OMB), and the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) divide the fiscal effect of § 32 into two separate budgetary categories—(a) revenue reductions (the nonrefundable portion that offsets positive income tax liability) and (b) direct spending outlays under 31 U.S.C. § 1324 (the refundable portion exceeding tax liability). In this report, both the revenue-offset component and the refundable-outlay component are included within the boundary of § 32, and always reported separately to prevent double counting or conflation.

## 3.2 Current Law (As of October 31, 2026)

### Distinction Between Enacted Law, Operative Law, and Expired Temporary Regimes
As of October 31, 2026, the operative statutory law governing the EITC for taxable year 2026 is codified in **26 U.S.C. § 32**, as permanently amended through the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2016 (Division Q, Protecting Americans from Tax Hikes [PATH] Act of 2015, Pub. L. No. 114-113), An Act to provide for reconciliation pursuant to titles II and V of the concurrent resolution on the budget for fiscal year 2018 (commonly known as the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act [TCJA], Pub. L. No. 115-97), the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021 (Taxpayer Certainty and Disaster Tax Relief Act of 2020, Pub. L. No. 116-260), and the permanent EITC structural amendments of the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 (ARPA, Pub. L. No. 117-2, §§ 9622–9624).

Crucially, researchers and practitioners must distinguish:
1. **Operative Law for Tax Year 2026 (Returns generally filed in Calendar Year 2027):** Governed by the permanent statutory structure of 26 U.S.C. § 32 and the inflation-adjusted dollar amounts formally published by the IRS in **Revenue Procedure 2025-32, § 3.06** (issued October 2025; direct inspection verified).
2. **Operative Law for Tax Year 2025 (Returns filed during the 2026 Filing Season):** Governed by the same underlying statutory text in 26 U.S.C. § 32, but using the Tax Year 2025 inflation-adjusted dollar parameters published in **Revenue Procedure 2024-40, § 3.06** (issued October 2024; e.g., maximum credit of $8,046 for three or more children, $649 for childless workers, and investment income limit of $11,950). A tax return filed during calendar year 2026 typically reports Tax Year 2025 parameters, whereas payroll, withholding, and tax-year transactions occurring on October 31, 2026 are governed by Tax Year 2026 parameters.
3. **Permanent TCJA Chained CPI-U Indexing (No 2025 Sunset):** Unlike the temporary individual income tax rate cuts and the temporary Child Tax Credit expansion in § 24(h) enacted by Pub. L. No. 115-97 (which carried a December 31, 2025 sunset date prior to 2025 legislative debates), the TCJA amendment switching EITC inflation indexing from traditional CPI-U to **Chained CPI-U (C-CPI-U)** under 26 U.S.C. §§ 1(f)(3), 1(f)(6), and 32(j)(1) was enacted **permanently** for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2017 (Pub. L. No. 115-97, § 11002(d), 131 Stat. 2054, 2060).
4. **Expired Temporary ARPA Provisions vs. Permanent ARPA Provisions:** Under ARPA (Pub. L. No. 117-2), the temporary childless-worker expansion in § 32(n) (lowering the minimum age to 19, 24 for specified students, and 18 for qualified former foster/homeless youth, eliminating the age 65 cap, and increasing the childless credit percentage to 15.3%) applied **only to taxable years beginning in 2021** and remains expired in 2026. Conversely, three other ARPA EITC changes were enacted **permanently** for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2020 and remain operative in 2026:
   - Repeal of the rule barring a taxpayer who has a qualifying child lacking a valid SSN from claiming the *childless* EITC (§ 32(c)(1)(F), amended by Pub. L. No. 117-2, § 9622).
   - Expansion of the separated-spouse exception to the joint-return requirement (§ 32(d)(2), amended by Pub. L. No. 117-2, § 9623).
   - Increase in the base § 32(i) excessive investment income threshold from $2,200 (indexed to $3,650 in 2020) to $10,000 for 2021, indexed for inflation after 2021 ($12,200 in Tax Year 2026) (Pub. L. No. 117-2, § 9624).
5. **Proposed 119th Congress Legislation (Non-Enacted Proposals):** Bills introduced in the 119th Congress (2025–2026), such as H.R. 2764 (*Tax Cut for Workers Act of 2025*, which proposed reinstating expanded childless EITC rules for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025), are legislative bills and are **not enacted law** as of Rev. Proc. 2025-32 and the October 31, 2026 snapshot.

### Core Statutory Computation Mechanics (Tax Year 2026)
Under 26 U.S.C. § 32(a) and (b), the EITC is computed along a trapezoid with three distinct regions based on two income measures—**Earned Income (EI)** and **Adjusted Gross Income (AGI)**:

1. **Phase-In Region ($\text{EI} < \text{Earned Income Amount}$):**
   $$\text{Credit} = \text{Credit Percentage} \times \text{EI}$$
2. **Maximum-Credit Plateau ($\text{Earned Income Amount} \le \text{EI}$ and $\max(\text{EI}, \text{AGI}) \le \text{Threshold Phaseout Amount}$):**
   $$\text{Credit} = \text{Credit Percentage} \times \text{Earned Income Amount} = \text{Maximum Credit}$$
3. **Phase-Out Region ($\max(\text{EI}, \text{AGI}) > \text{Threshold Phaseout Amount}$):**
   $$\text{Credit} = \max\Big(0,\; \text{Maximum Credit} - \text{Phaseout Percentage} \times \big(\max(\text{EI}, \text{AGI}) - \text{Threshold Phaseout Amount}\big)\Big)$$

Note that under IRS administrative guidance (Form 1040 Instructions and 26 U.S.C. § 32(f)), the actual credit claimed on a return is looked up in official $50-bracket EIC Tables based on the midpoint of each $50 income bracket rather than computed to the exact penny by continuous multiplication.
