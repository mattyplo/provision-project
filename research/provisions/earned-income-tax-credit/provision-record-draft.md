# Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) — audited provision record (draft)

**Provision:** 26 U.S.C. §32 · **Research snapshot:** October 31, 2026 · **Compiled:** November 16, 2026 · **Status:** Draft for editorial review. This is an evidence synthesis, not legal or tax advice. Each source supports the specific claim alongside it; research findings are not treated as statutory facts.

## At a glance
The **Earned Income Tax Credit** is a **refundable federal income-tax credit** tied to earned income, family circumstances and filing requirements. As earned income increases, the credit generally rises, reaches a maximum and then phases out. A qualifying worker can receive the refundable portion even if income tax liability is zero. [26 USC §32(a)–(c)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-124/t26/s32/).

**Why does it exist?** In 1975 the Senate Finance Committee described three objectives: offsetting part of low-income workers' payroll-tax burden **without reducing Social Security tax receipts**, encouraging work, and reducing welfare dependency. Those are **the committee's stated aims**, not proof that the policy accomplished them. [S. Rep. 94-36, printed pp. 10–11, 32–35](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/tax1.pdf).

## When and how did it enter the code?
Congress enacted the original **temporary** refundable credit in the [Tax Reduction Act of 1975, P.L. 94-12 §204](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-89/pdf/STATUTE-89-Pg26.pdf), as former **§43**. It initially equaled 10% of up to $4,000 in earned income (maximum **$400**) and applied to tax years beginning in **1975** under §209(b). It was extended for subsequent years and made permanent by [P.L. 95-600 §103 (1978)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-92/pdf/STATUTE-92-Pg2763.pdf). [P.L. 98-369 §471(c)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-124/t26/s32/) renumbered it as **§32** in 1984.

Selected material changes (not an exhaustive amendment inventory): **1986** expansion and indexing (P.L. 99-514 §§104,111); **1990** family-size differentiation (P.L. 101-508 §11111); **1993** expansion and eligibility for workers without qualifying children (P.L. 103-66 §13131); **2001** marriage-related reforms (P.L. 107-16 §303); **2009** higher credit for three or more children and marriage-related changes (P.L. 111-5 §1002); **2015** permanence of certain enhancements (P.L. 114-113 div. Q §103); **2021** temporary one-year childless-worker expansion (P.L. 117-2 §9621, §32(n)). Effective dates differ by law; see [legislative history](legislative-history-audit.md) and [bounded statutory audit](bounded-statutory-audit.md). The review of laws enacted through Oct. 31, 2026 found **no direct amendment to §32** after the inspected OLRC edition; indirect interactions are not exhaustively certified. [October cutoff audit](october-2026-cutoff-closeout.md).

## Who qualifies?
Eligibility depends on **earned income and adjusted gross income**, filing status, valid taxpayer SSNs, residency and other conditions; qualifying children can raise the credit. Workers **without** qualifying children may qualify under additional age and residency rules. Qualifying separated spouses are an exception to the general restriction on married taxpayers filing separately. A qualifying child who lacks the required SSN may be ineligible to count toward the higher child-based amount **without necessarily barring the taxpayer from a self-only credit**. Special rules concern shared children, combat-pay elections, clergy, disability income and prior disallowance. [26 USC §32](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-124/t26/s32/); [IRS EITC eligibility](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/individuals/earned-income-tax-credit/who-qualifies-for-the-earned-income-tax-credit-eitc); [IRS Pub. 596 (2025 rules; not independently certified as TY2026 instructions)](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p596). See [eligibility exceptions](eligibility-exceptions-audit.md).

**Tax year 2026 maximum credit:** **$664** (0 qualifying children), **$4,427** (1), **$7,316** (2), **$8,231** (3+). Investment-income exclusion applies when investment income **exceeds $12,200**. These are **2026 IRS parameters**, not amounts everyone receives. [IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32 §3.06](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2025-45_IRB).

**Regulatory proposal, not established operative restriction:** An August 2026 Treasury/IRS proposal (REG-119882-25) would apply federal-public-benefit immigration-status restrictions to the **refunded portion** of EITC. An October hearing was cancelled; CRS described the rule as proposed on Oct. 26. No final rule was identified by the Oct. 31 cutoff in the targeted search. A draft Schedule 3-A and later IRS web guidance are not proof of finalization. [Proposed notice, 91 FR 53812](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-08-20/pdf/FR-2026-08-20.pdf); [regulatory audit](prwora-regulatory-status.md).

## Who uses it?
IRS reports approximately **23 million TY2023 EITC returns and $64 billion** in its Statistics of Income series. A separate IRS state-level processing table reports approximately **24 million TY2024 claims and $69.6 billion**, processed in 2025. These are **different years and statistical products**; neither is an undifferentiated count of unique people or the FY2026 fiscal estimate. [IRS SOI](https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/individuals/earned-income-tax-credit/earned-income-tax-credit-statistics); [IRS state processing statistics](https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/eitc-central/statistics-for-tax-returns-with-the-earned-income-tax-credit-eitc).

For TY2022, IRS reports a **national 80.8% participation estimate using ACS-linked data**, versus **78% using a CPS-based method**. Both are modeled participation measures with differing denominators, **not** directly counted shares of all eligible individuals. [IRS participation methodology and table](https://www.irs.gov/tax-professionals/eitc-central/eitc-participation-rate-by-state).

## What is its estimated fiscal effect?
[JCT JCX-45-25, Table 1](https://www.jct.gov/getattachment/fe301c56-965c-48c9-ac06-52b7b6765b05/x-45-25.pdf) estimates **FY2026 $67.2 billion** for the EITC tax expenditure, including a **$56.6 billion outlay component**. The **$10.6 billion difference** is **project arithmetic on rounded published numbers**, not a separately published JCT line item. This JCT forecast reflects its stated baseline and legislative cutoff, **not** observed FY2026 spending or a fully refreshed forecast of all laws enacted through Oct. 31.

**Administration:** GAO reports IRS estimated EITC improper-payment rates of **33.5% in FY2023** and **27.3% in FY2024**. **Improper payment does not mean proven fraud**. [GAO-26-108044](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-108044/index.html).

## Who benefits economically? What does research show?
**Legal recipient ≠ final economic incidence.** Workers and households may receive credit payments; wages and labor supply can adjust, potentially affecting employers and other workers. **No defensible nationwide allocation of ultimate benefits was established by this audit. Status: Unknown / insufficient evidence.** The following **researcher-reported** results are preserved despite disagreement.

### Employment
- **Eissa & Liebman (1995 working paper / 1996 published article):** +**1.4 pp** in the working-paper abstract (post period 1988–89) versus **up to +2.8 pp** in the published study (1988–90). Published Tables III–IV show specification and subgroup sensitivity. TRA1986 changed other policies alongside EITC; the result is **not exclusively identified as an EITC-only effect**. [Published study](https://faculty.georgetown.edu/noe/qje_596.pdf); [NBER working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w5158).
- **Schanzenbach & Strain (2021):** pooled annual-employment estimates around **+2.6 to +3.3 pp** per **$1,000 increase in maximum available credit**, in selected specifications; annual vs weekly employment, education sample and controls materially affect results. Their paper reproduces estimates resembling Kleven's earlier work under matched specifications. [Published paper](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/epdf/10.1086/713494).
- **Kleven (2024):** author-hosted revision examines **432 specifications**; mean estimated effects **−0.32 pp** for non-1993 reforms and **+4.02 pp** for the 1993 reform. He argues the exceptional episode may be confounded by welfare reform and macroeconomic conditions. These are **means across a specification grid**, not a single universal causal effect. [Author revision](https://www.henrikkleven.com/uploads/3/7/3/1/37310663/kleven_eitc_may2024.pdf).

**Interpretation:** There are both positive and skeptical employment findings; different samples, counterfactuals, outcomes and controls explain some of the divergence. **No pooled causal employment estimate adopted.** [Employment table audit](employment-robustness-audit.md).

### Wages and incidence
- **Rothstein (2010):** under preferred structural-model assumptions, $1 of EITC spending yields approximately **$0.73 in higher after-tax worker income**. The published supplement reports an alternative income-effect scenario that **reverses the modeled net employer/worker transfer direction**. These are **simulations**, not observed employer capture. [Article](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.2.1.177); [supplement](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles/materials/705).
- **Leigh (2010):** state-policy wage regressions report associations of a **10% more generous EITC** with roughly **5% lower wages among high-school dropouts** and **2% among high-school graduates**, but no significant college-graduate wage effect. The author examines composition changes; remaining identification limitations preclude a nationwide incidence fraction. [Full paper](https://docs.iza.org/dp4960.pdf).

### Infant health
- **Hoynes, Miller & Simon:** the **2012 NBER working paper** reports **6.7–10.8%** relative low-birth-weight reductions per $1,000 EITC increase in its specified population; the **2015 published paper** reports about **2–3% relative** per $1,000 policy-induced after-tax income. **These are versions of the same research, not independent corroborations.** [Working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18206); [published article](https://gspp.berkeley.edu/archived/files/research/pdf/Hoynes-Miller-Simon-AEJPolicy-Feb-2015.pdf).
- **Dench & Joyce (2020)** reproduce some key subgroup contrasts but report nonzero parity-placebo differences and question the causal identification. The original authors **dispute the critique**. Both the criticism and response are retained; **causality remains contested**. [Critique](https://www.danieldench.com/static/pdfs/published/HE2.pdf); [authors' response](https://gspp.berkeley.edu/assets/uploads/research/pdf/DJ_response_JHE_012519.pdf); [table-level audit](empirical-robustness-tables.md).

## Evidence and editorial limitations
- **Verified statutory/administrative facts:** tied to enacted text, IRS, JCT and GAO sources, with tax-year/fiscal-year and estimate-vintage distinctions.
- **Researcher-reported empirical findings:** attributed to their authors; selected robustness tables were inspected but **underlying regressions were not independently reproduced**. Do not read “statistically significant” as “causally settled.”
- **Unknown:** national ultimate incidence split; complete causal attribution of employment and infant-health effects; every TY2026 special-case eligibility interaction.
- **Historical limits:** selected major amendments and a bounded direct-§32 cutoff, not a claim to every indirect tax interaction.
- **Regulatory limits:** targeted negative final-rule search, not a certified exhaustive docket inventory.
