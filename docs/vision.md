# Vision

## The Problem

The U.S. federal tax code is the product of decades of legislation, amendment, compromise, policy experimentation, and changing economic conditions. Individual provisions can be easy to find in current law while being much harder to understand in context.

A reader may be able to determine what a provision does today without being able to answer more fundamental questions: When did it enter the tax code? What problem was Congress trying to address? What did the original provision look like? How has it changed? Who is legally eligible for it? Who ultimately receives its economic benefit? How much revenue does it cost or raise?

The evidence needed to answer those questions is also fragmented across statutory text, Public Laws, congressional reports, Joint Committee on Taxation publications, Treasury and IRS materials, budget estimates, government oversight reports, academic research, and historical records.

The Provision Project exists to connect those pieces.

## The Provision Project

The Provision Project is a public, source-backed explorer of provisions in the U.S. federal tax code.

For each provision, the project seeks to answer a consistent set of questions:

- What does it do?
- Who qualifies for it?
- When and how did it enter federal tax law?
- Why was it created?
- How has it changed over time?
- Who economically benefits from it?
- What is its estimated fiscal effect?
- What evidence supports those conclusions?

The aim is not simply to explain current tax law. It is to make the history and evolution of the law navigable.

A mature provision record should allow a reader to move from the rule as it exists today backward through major amendments to its enactment, the contemporaneous explanation for its creation, and the evidence available about its effects.

## What Is a Provision?

For purposes of this project, a provision is a distinct rule or set of related rules in federal tax law that materially changes the tax treatment of a defined activity, transaction, asset, source of income, taxpayer, or group of taxpayers.

A provision does not necessarily correspond one-to-one with a section of the Internal Revenue Code.

One Code section may contain several substantively different rules. Conversely, a recognizable tax policy may depend on multiple sections, definitions, limitations, or related rules.

The project's unit of analysis should therefore follow the substance of the tax treatment rather than the numbering structure of the Code alone.

This is a working definition. One purpose of the initial research phase is to test and refine it against real provisions.

## Principles

### Start with the law

Claims about what a provision does should begin with current statutory text and, where relevant, regulations and other authoritative legal material.

### Trace claims to sources

Important factual claims should be supported by identifiable sources. Wherever possible, the project should preserve a trail from a summarized claim back to the underlying evidence.

### Prefer primary and nonpartisan official sources

The project should prioritize the U.S. Code, Public Laws, congressional committee reports and legislative materials, the Joint Committee on Taxation, Treasury and IRS, the Congressional Budget Office, and the Government Accountability Office.

Academic research and other high-quality secondary sources can add important economic and historical analysis. Advocacy organizations, industry groups, and commentary may also provide useful arguments or evidence, but their role and perspective should be identified.

### Separate different kinds of claims

The project should clearly distinguish among:

- statutory and legal facts;
- contemporaneous explanations and stated legislative purposes;
- government revenue and distributional estimates;
- empirical economic research;
- arguments made by supporters, critics, affected industries, and advocacy groups; and
- the project's own technical inferences.

A plausible explanation should not be presented as documented legislative intent.

### Distinguish eligibility from economic benefit

The taxpayer legally entitled to a deduction, credit, exclusion, exemption, deferral, preferential rate, or other treatment is not necessarily the only person who economically benefits from it.

Where credible evidence exists, the project should distinguish legal eligibility from economic incidence and distribution.

### Preserve uncertainty

Historical records and economic evidence will not always support a definitive answer. Conflicting estimates, methodological limitations, missing evidence, and uncertainty should be visible rather than resolved through unsupported assumptions.

### Do not embed the verdict in the research

The project should not classify provisions as "loopholes," "good," "bad," "wasteful," or "corrupt" as factual conclusions.

Supporters and critics may use those descriptions, and their arguments can be documented and attributed. The project's job is to expose the law, history, evidence, tradeoffs, and competing claims so readers can make their own judgments.

## Initial Scope

The initial scope is the U.S. federal tax code.

The first milestone is not comprehensive coverage. It is five deeply researched provisions chosen to test the project across different types of tax treatment, histories, beneficiaries, and available evidence.

Depth comes before breadth.

Those first records should be used to refine:

- the definition of a provision;
- the research methodology;
- source and citation standards;
- the provision record template;
- the underlying data model; and
- the requirements for the eventual public interface.

The research framework should be treated as versioned and revisable rather than assumed to be complete before the first provision is studied.

## Long-Term Vision

If the initial model works, The Provision Project can become a navigable map of the federal tax code.

A reader should be able to explore the code provision by provision, understand how each rule works today, who qualifies for it, who economically benefits, what its fiscal effect is, and trace how and why it entered the code and changed over time.

A provision record should connect those dimensions:

**Current law → eligibility and mechanics → beneficiaries and economic incidence → fiscal effect → enactment and original rationale → major amendments → underlying sources**

The interface should make it possible to understand what the tax code contains and how its provisions relate to the people, activities, industries, and economic outcomes they affect, while preserving the history of how particular rules arrived there and evolved.

Over time, structured provision records could also make broader questions easier to explore: which eras produced particular kinds of tax provisions, how frequently provisions are amended or extended, which industries or taxpayer groups are affected by related rules, and how official estimates of fiscal effects change over time.

The long-term ambition is not to replace the primary sources. It is to make them easier to discover, connect, understand, and interrogate.
