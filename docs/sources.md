# Research Sources

## Purpose

This document is the practical source map for The Provision Project.

The methodology defines what evidence is required for a claim. This document answers the next question: **where should we look for that evidence?**

The goal is not to create a single rigid source ranking. Different sources answer different questions. Statutory text is the starting point for current law; committee reports may be better evidence of a contemporaneous legislative rationale; IRS data may be better evidence of actual use; and empirical economic research may be necessary to study incidence.

Whenever possible, research should begin with primary and nonpartisan official sources and then expand to secondary sources where they add evidence, context, interpretation, or competing analysis.

Links below are starting points, not substitutes for pinpoint citations in provision records.

## Quick Research Path

For a new provision, a useful default sequence is:

1. **U.S. Code** — identify the current statutory rule and notes.
2. **Public Laws / Statutes at Large** — locate enactment and material amendments.
3. **Congressional legislative materials** — committee reports, conference reports, bill history, and related documents.
4. **JCT** — find technical explanations, Bluebooks, revenue estimates, tax expenditure estimates, and distributional analyses.
5. **Treasury / IRS** — find regulations, administrative guidance, tax expenditure estimates, tax data, and implementation material.
6. **CBO / GAO / CRS** — look for budgetary, evaluative, implementation, and contextual analysis.
7. **Academic and other empirical research** — investigate economic incidence, behavioral effects, and questions official sources do not resolve.
8. **Advocacy, industry, and other interested-party sources** — document arguments, implementation experience, and disputes, with attribution.

The sequence is a research heuristic, not an evidentiary ranking.

# 1. Current Statutory Law

## U.S. Code — Office of the Law Revision Counsel

**Primary use:** Current codified statutory text.

**Start here:** https://uscode.house.gov/

Title 26 contains the Internal Revenue Code. The Office of the Law Revision Counsel's online Code is the preferred starting point for determining the current statutory language and identifying statutory notes, amendments, effective dates, cross-references, and related provisions.

Useful material may include:

- current section text;
- source credits;
- amendment notes;
- effective-date notes;
- transition provisions;
- references in text;
- cross-references; and
- historical and statutory notes.

### Research caution

Current codified text tells us what the law says now. It does not, by itself, reconstruct what the law said at a particular historical moment or establish why Congress enacted a rule.

For historical changes, follow the section's amendment history back to the relevant Public Laws and enacted text.

---

# 2. Enacted Laws and Historical Statutory Text

## GovInfo — Public and Private Laws / Statutes at Large

**Primary use:** Authoritative enacted legislation and historical statutory text.

**Start here:** https://www.govinfo.gov/

The United States Statutes at Large is the permanent chronological collection of laws and resolutions enacted by Congress. Public Laws should be used to establish what Congress enacted, when it was enacted, and the precise language of amendments.

Useful collections include:

- Public and Private Laws;
- United States Statutes at Large;
- Congressional Bills;
- Congressional Reports;
- Congressional Record; and
- statute compilations and related legislative material.

### What to capture

For an enactment or material amendment, record where available:

- Public Law number;
- bill number;
- enactment date;
- Statutes at Large citation;
- section of the Public Law;
- affected Code section;
- effective date; and
- relevant statutory language.

### Research caution

GovInfo's statute compilations can be useful research and drafting aids, but GovInfo itself identifies those compilations as unofficial. The enacted Public Law, Statutes at Large, and applicable U.S. Code text should support claims about the law.

---

# 3. Legislative History

## Congress.gov

**Primary use:** Legislative chronology and links among bills, actions, reports, amendments, votes, and related documents.

**Start here:** https://www.congress.gov/

Use Congress.gov to reconstruct the path of legislation and locate:

- introduced and subsequent bill texts;
- bill actions;
- committee referrals;
- amendments;
- committee reports;
- conference reports;
- roll-call information;
- related bills; and
- Congressional Record references.

Congress.gov is particularly useful as an index into the legislative record. For historical documents, GovInfo may provide the underlying official publication.

## Committee Reports and Conference Reports

**Primary use:** Contemporaneous explanations of legislation and strong evidence of documented legislative rationale.

Tax legislation will frequently involve:

- House Committee on Ways and Means;
- Senate Committee on Finance; and
- conference committees where House and Senate versions differ.

When explaining why a provision was created, prefer precise attribution:

> The House Ways and Means Committee stated...

rather than automatically converting a committee explanation into a claim about the motives of Congress as a whole.

## Congressional Record

**Primary use:** Floor debate, statements by legislators, explanations of amendments, and the surrounding political or policy debate.

**Research caution:** A statement by an individual member of Congress establishes that person's stated position. It does not automatically establish collective congressional intent.

# 4. Joint Committee on Taxation

## Joint Committee on Taxation (JCT)

**Primary uses:**

- technical explanations of tax legislation;
- legislative tax history;
- revenue estimates;
- tax expenditure estimates;
- distributional analysis;
- descriptions of proposals;
- Bluebooks / general explanations of enacted tax legislation; and
- methodological material.

**Start here:** https://www.jct.gov/

**Publication search:** https://www.jct.gov/search/

JCT is one of the project's most important recurring sources. Its publication database contains thousands of documents organized into categories including Bluebooks, revenue estimates, tax expenditures, technical explanations, markup documents, macroeconomic analysis, and hearing documents.

### Technical explanations and markup documents

Use these to understand the mechanics of proposed or enacted tax changes and the context in which Congress considered them.

### Bluebooks

JCT's General Explanations of Tax Legislation Enacted in a Congress can be particularly useful for reconstructing enacted tax changes and their background.

A Bluebook is a later JCT explanation, however, and should not silently be treated as contemporaneous evidence of legislative intent. Where purpose matters, compare it with committee reports and other contemporaneous material.

### Revenue estimates

JCT provides the official congressional revenue estimates for tax legislation considered by Congress.

Revenue estimates generally compare a proposal against a revenue baseline over a budget window and incorporate anticipated taxpayer behavioral responses while conventionally holding the overall size of the economy fixed unless a separate macroeconomic analysis is performed.

Capture the specific JCT document number, proposal version, date, budget window, and baseline context.

### Tax expenditures

JCT generally publishes annual estimates of federal tax expenditures.

These are valuable for estimating the scale of many existing provisions and sometimes include distributions of selected tax expenditures by income class.

Do not equate a tax expenditure estimate with the revenue that would necessarily be raised by repealing a provision. Preserve JCT's definitions and methodological limitations.

### Distributional analysis

JCT sometimes publishes analyses of how tax-law changes affect taxpayers across income groups.

Record the population, income concept, year, tax measure, and assumptions rather than reducing a distribution table to a single beneficiary statement.

# 5. Department of the Treasury

## Office of Tax Policy

**Primary uses:**

- tax expenditure estimates;
- tax policy analysis;
- administration proposals;
- technical material;
- international tax documents; and
- historical policy reports.

**Start here:** https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/tax-policy

## Treasury Tax Expenditures

**Start here:** https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/tax-policy/tax-expenditures

Treasury publishes annual tax expenditure estimates, including downloadable tables and historical editions.

Treasury defines tax expenditures as revenue losses attributable to specified exclusions, exemptions, deductions, credits, preferential rates, or deferrals.

Treasury's own methodological cautions matter. Its tax expenditure estimates generally assume taxpayers do not change the underlying activities they undertake in response to eliminating the provision, and individual tax expenditures should not simply be added together because provisions interact.

When using a Treasury estimate, preserve:

- fiscal year edition;
- provision name;
- years covered;
- current-revenue or present-value measure where applicable;
- baseline/reference-law assumptions; and
- methodological notes.

## Treasury Regulations and Decisions

Treasury regulations interpret and implement provisions of the Internal Revenue Code.

Final and temporary regulations, Treasury Decisions, and related rulemaking material can be essential where statutory text alone does not establish how a provision operates in practice.

When a provision record relies on a regulation, distinguish the statutory rule from the regulatory interpretation or implementation.

---

# 6. Internal Revenue Service

## IRS Guidance

**Primary uses:**

- Revenue Rulings;
- Revenue Procedures;
- Notices;
- Announcements;
- Treasury Decisions as published through the Internal Revenue Bulletin;
- written determinations;
- forms and instructions; and
- implementation guidance.

**Start here:** https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-guidance

## Internal Revenue Bulletin

**Start here:** https://www.irs.gov/internal-revenue-bulletins

The IRS describes the Internal Revenue Bulletin as its authoritative instrument for announcing official rulings and procedures and publishing Treasury Decisions and other official material.

The IRB can help determine how the IRS interprets or administers a provision.

### Research caution

Different forms of IRS material carry different legal weight. IRS FAQs, press releases, publications, and explanatory webpages may be useful for understanding administration or locating authoritative material, but they should not automatically be treated as equivalent to statutes, regulations, or published guidance.

The IRS specifically cautions that FAQs generally cannot be relied upon as legal authority.

Written determinations, including private letter rulings and similar materials, generally may not be used or cited as precedent under 26 U.S.C. § 6110(k)(3). They may still be useful for understanding IRS treatment of a specific matter or as research leads, but they should not be treated as generally authoritative interpretive support.

## Statistics of Income (SOI)

**Primary uses:**

- observed taxpayer use;
- number of returns;
- amounts reported;
- income distributions;
- business statistics;
- geographic statistics;
- public-use microdata; and
- historical tax statistics.

**Start here:** https://www.irs.gov/statistics/soi-tax-stats-products-publications-and-papers

SOI is likely to be one of the project's most important sources for moving from **who is legally eligible** to **who actually uses a provision**.

Available products include historical tables, individual and corporation reports, ZIP Code and county data, public-use microdata, SOI Bulletins, and specialized studies.

### Research caution

A tax-return field does not always map cleanly to a provision. Aggregated tables may combine provisions, omit nonfilers, suppress cells, or reflect reporting rules rather than economic incidence. An amount reported for a deduction, exclusion, credit, or other tax item also does not necessarily equal the taxpayer's reduction in tax liability.

Always document the tax year, filing population, variable definition, unit of observation, and known limitations.

---

# 7. Congressional Budget Office

## Congressional Budget Office (CBO)

**Primary uses:**

- federal budget baselines;
- budgetary and economic analysis;
- distributional work;
- analyses of tax and spending policy; and
- methodological context.

**Start here:** https://www.cbo.gov/

JCT generally performs the congressional revenue estimation for tax legislation, while CBO's broader budget and economic work can provide baseline, macroeconomic, and program context.

Do not attribute a JCT revenue estimate to CBO merely because it appears in a CBO cost estimate or budget document.

---

# 8. Government Accountability Office

## Government Accountability Office (GAO)

**Primary uses:**

- program and policy evaluation;
- tax administration;
- implementation;
- compliance;
- tax expenditures;
- data limitations;
- oversight findings; and
- evaluations of whether policies are meeting stated objectives.

**Start here:** https://www.gao.gov/

GAO reports can be especially useful when the project's question is not merely what a provision costs, but how it operates in practice and what evidence exists about its effectiveness, administration, or beneficiaries.

Preserve GAO's scope and methodology. Findings about a sampled population, agency program, or historical period should not automatically be generalized beyond that population or period.

---

# 9. Congressional Research Service

## Congressional Research Service (CRS)

**Primary uses:**

- nonpartisan background;
- legislative history summaries;
- explanations of complex tax rules;
- synthesis of policy debates; and
- references that lead to primary sources.

**Start here:** https://crsreports.congress.gov/

CRS reports can be excellent orientation material, especially when a provision has a complicated history.

They are generally secondary rather than primary evidence for statutory enactment or legislative intent. Use their citations to locate underlying statutes, reports, estimates, and empirical work whenever practical.

---

# 10. Federal Register and Regulations

## Federal Register

**Primary uses:**

- proposed regulations;
- final regulations;
- notices;
- agency explanations;
- effective dates;
- agency discussion of comments, docket references, and rulemaking history.

**Start here:** https://www.federalregister.gov/

## eCFR

**Primary use:** Current, updated editorial compilation of CFR text.

**Start here:** https://www.ecfr.gov/

eCFR is an excellent current-law research tool, but it is not the official annual edition of the Code of Federal Regulations. Preserve the page's current-as-of date when relevant. Where historical or version-specific regulatory text matters, use annual CFR editions together with the relevant Federal Register amendment history.

For tax provisions whose real-world operation depends heavily on regulation, preserve both the Code citation and the relevant regulation citation.

Proposed regulations should be clearly distinguished from final rules.

---

# 11. Courts and Judicial Interpretation

Court decisions may be necessary where a provision's meaning, scope, constitutionality, or administration has been materially shaped by litigation.

Potential sources include:

- U.S. Supreme Court;
- U.S. Courts of Appeals;
- U.S. Tax Court;
- U.S. Court of Federal Claims; and
- federal district courts.

For provision records, judicial decisions should be included when they materially affect the current rule, eligibility, historical development, or practical application.

Do not treat litigation as necessary merely because cases exist. Include it when it changes or materially clarifies the story of the provision.

---

# 12. Academic and Empirical Research

**Primary uses:**

- economic incidence;
- behavioral responses;
- capitalization effects;
- labor and investment effects;
- distribution;
- historical analysis; and
- causal evaluation.

Useful research may come from:

- peer-reviewed journals;
- NBER working papers;
- university researchers;
- recognized economic and legal research institutions; and
- replication datasets.

Academic work is particularly important when the legal recipient of a tax benefit may differ from the person or group that ultimately captures its economic value.

### Evaluation questions

For empirical research, record or assess:

- population studied;
- time period;
- policy variation used;
- identification strategy;
- outcome measured;
- assumptions;
- robustness;
- whether the study examines the same provision and version of law;
- whether results have been replicated or challenged; and
- whether the authors disclose relevant conflicts or funding.

A prestigious source does not eliminate the need to inspect methodology.

---

# 13. Advocacy, Industry, Think Tanks, and Interested Parties

These sources can be valuable for understanding:

- arguments for and against a provision;
- industry-specific mechanics;
- implementation problems;
- proposed reforms;
- stakeholder behavior; and
- leads to data or primary documents.

Examples may include policy organizations, trade associations, labor organizations, taxpayer groups, professional associations, advocacy organizations, and affected firms.

These sources should generally be used as **attributed evidence** for arguments or as leads to independently verifiable evidence.

For example:

> The association argues that...

is different from:

> The provision causes...

The project's job is not to exclude interested parties. It is to make their role and evidentiary basis visible.

---

# 14. News and Investigative Reporting

High-quality journalism can be useful for:

- discovering provisions worth investigating;
- identifying affected taxpayers or industries;
- locating documents;
- reconstructing political and legislative context;
- surfacing implementation consequences; and
- identifying disputes requiring further research.

Whenever a news report relies on a public document, dataset, court filing, disclosure, or government estimate that can be obtained directly, the project should generally cite the underlying source for the factual claim and use the reporting for context or original reporting that cannot be independently reproduced.

---

# 15. Historical Sources

Older provisions may require sources that predate modern online congressional databases.

Potential sources include:

- historical Statutes at Large;
- historical committee reports;
- Congressional Record and predecessor publications;
- JCT archives;
- Treasury annual reports;
- historical IRS publications;
- presidential messages and budget documents;
- archival congressional material;
- legal treatises and law reviews; and
- scholarly histories.

Historical claims should identify whether the source is contemporaneous with enactment or a later reconstruction.

When Congress.gov or GovInfo does not provide an older committee report, hearing, or related legislative document, use practical fallbacks such as Library of Congress collections and catalog records, the Congressional Serial Set, or other institutional legislative-history collections, then cite the underlying document where obtainable.

# 16. Source Selection by Question

| Research question | Preferred starting sources |
| --- | --- |
| What does the provision do today? | U.S. Code; Treasury regulations; authoritative IRS guidance; relevant case law |
| When was it enacted? | Public Law; Statutes at Large; U.S. Code historical/amendment notes |
| What did the original law say? | Public Law; Statutes at Large |
| Why was it enacted? | Contemporaneous committee/conference reports; JCT materials; Congressional Record; administration materials |
| How has it changed? | U.S. Code amendment notes; Public Laws; JCT explanations; committee reports |
| Who is legally eligible? | U.S. Code; regulations; authoritative guidance |
| Who actually uses it? | IRS SOI; Treasury/JCT/GAO data; administrative datasets |
| Who receives the direct tax benefit? | IRS/SOI data where tax-liability effects are actually measured; JCT/Treasury distributional analysis; empirical research |
| Who ultimately benefits economically? | Empirical economic literature; official incidence analysis; transparent datasets |
| What is its fiscal effect? | JCT revenue/tax expenditure estimates; Treasury tax expenditure estimates; relevant CBO context |
| How is it administered? | IRS; Treasury; GAO; regulations; administrative data |
| What are the major arguments around it? | Committee hearings; stakeholder materials; think tanks; academic work; journalism, all attributed |

# 17. Minimum Source Record

Every source used in a provision record should preserve enough information to find and evaluate it later.

At minimum:

- **title**
- **author / issuing institution**
- **publication date**
- **document type**
- **document or report number**, when available
- **stable URL or locator**
- **page / section / table / line reference**, where practical
- **date accessed**, when appropriate
- **claim(s) supported**
- **source role** — e.g. statute, legislative history, estimate, empirical analysis, stakeholder argument
- **claim relationship** — e.g. direct support, contextual support, lead only, inference support

Where relevant, also capture:

- law or proposal version;
- tax year or fiscal years;
- population;
- baseline;
- estimate type;
- whether the evidence is contemporaneous or retrospective; and
- known limitations.

---

# 18. Source Roles

To keep evidence types visible, provision records should classify sources by role where practical.

Suggested roles:

- **statute**
- **regulation**
- **administrative guidance**
- **judicial interpretation**
- **legislative history**
- **government estimate**
- **government data**
- **government analysis**
- **empirical research**
- **secondary synthesis**
- **stakeholder argument**
- **journalism**

A source may serve more than one role, but the claim should make clear how it is being used. Whether a source directly supports a claim, provides context, serves only as a research lead, or supports a project inference is a relationship between the source and the claim rather than a source role.

---

# 19. Research Discipline

A few rules should apply across all source categories:

1. **Prefer the underlying source.** If a report quotes a Public Law, retrieve the Public Law. If journalism cites a JCT estimate, retrieve the JCT estimate.
2. **Do not confuse authority with relevance.** Statutory text is authoritative about the law but may tell us little about economic incidence.
3. **Preserve time context.** A correct estimate from 2017 is still a 2017 estimate.
4. **Preserve population context.** Evidence about corporations, itemizers, homeowners, or a particular income group should not silently become a claim about all taxpayers.
5. **Separate proposal from law.** A bill, campaign proposal, markup draft, or proposed regulation is not enacted current law.
6. **Separate stated rationale from inferred rationale.** Attribute statements and label project inference.
7. **Check whether a source is current.** Tax law changes frequently, and guidance, estimates, and explanatory pages may lag legislation.
8. **Record disagreement rather than hiding it.** Conflicting credible evidence belongs in the provision record.
9. **Do not use search-result summaries as evidence.** Open and inspect the underlying document.
10. **Make the trail reproducible.** Another researcher should be able to follow the citations and understand why the record says what it says.

# 20. Maintaining This Source Map

This file should evolve as the first five provision records expose better databases, archives, datasets, and research techniques.

When a new source proves repeatedly useful, add it here with:

- what it is;
- what questions it is good for;
- its authority or evidentiary role;
- important limitations; and
- a stable starting URL.

The purpose is not to catalog every tax resource. It is to build a reliable research map for answering The Provision Project's recurring questions.
