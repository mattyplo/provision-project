# Research Methodology

## Purpose

The Provision Project is intended to make provisions of the U.S. federal tax code understandable, traceable, and comparable without substituting the project's judgment for the evidence.

This methodology defines how provision records should be researched, what kinds of claims the project may make, how those claims should be supported, and how uncertainty should be represented.

It is a working methodology. The first five provision records are intended to test it. Where real research exposes weaknesses, the methodology should be revised rather than forcing the evidence into a predetermined structure.

## Core Research Questions

Every provision record should investigate the same core questions:

1. What does the provision do?
2. Who legally qualifies for it?
3. When and how did it enter federal tax law?
4. Why was it created?
5. How has it changed over time?
6. Who benefits from it, and what evidence supports that conclusion?
7. What is its estimated fiscal effect?
8. How confident are we in each answer?

A complete record does not require a definitive answer to every question. "Unknown," "insufficient evidence," or a documented disagreement may be the most accurate result.

## Unit of Analysis

The unit of analysis is a provision rather than necessarily a section of the Internal Revenue Code.

A provision is a distinct rule or set of related rules in federal tax law that materially changes the tax treatment of a defined activity, transaction, asset, source of income, taxpayer, or group of taxpayers.

One Code section may contain multiple substantively different provisions. A recognizable tax policy may also depend on multiple Code sections, definitions, limitations, transition rules, or related provisions.

Researchers should define the boundaries of the provision at the beginning of a record and document important related provisions rather than assuming Code numbering defines the correct unit of analysis.

## Research Workflow

Research should generally proceed through the following stages.

### 1. Identify the provision

Establish:

- a working name;
- current statutory citation or citations;
- the substantive tax treatment being studied;
- related provisions that may affect its operation; and
- the boundaries of the research record.

### 2. Establish current law

Determine how the provision operates under current law.

Document:

- the relevant statutory language;
- important definitions;
- eligibility requirements;
- limitations, thresholds, phaseouts, elections, and exceptions;
- interactions with other provisions; and
- relevant effective or expiration dates.

Where interpretation depends materially on regulations, authoritative administrative guidance, or case law, those sources should be identified separately from the statutory text.

### 3. Locate the origin

Identify the legislation that first enacted the provision or its recognizable predecessor.

Whenever possible, record:

- Public Law number;
- bill number;
- enactment date;
- original Code section;
- effective date;
- original statutory language; and
- contemporaneous legislative materials.

A modern Code citation should not be assumed to identify the true historical origin of the policy.

### 4. Establish the stated rationale

Search contemporaneous legislative evidence for explanations of why the provision was enacted.

Preferred evidence may include:

- House and Senate committee reports;
- conference reports;
- contemporaneous Joint Committee on Taxation explanations;
- official Treasury or administration materials associated with the proposal;
- Congressional Record statements, with the speaker and context identified; and
- other contemporaneous government documents.

The project should distinguish documented statements from later interpretations and from its own inference.

### 5. Trace material amendments

Identify amendments that materially changed eligibility, rates, thresholds, scope, duration, mechanics, or fiscal significance.

For each material change, record where possible:

**date → legislation → statutory change → stated rationale → estimated fiscal effect → sources**

Minor technical corrections need not receive the same treatment unless they materially affect how the provision operates.

### 6. Determine eligibility and observed use

Identify who may legally use the provision and, separately, who is observed using it.

Evidence of actual use may include IRS Statistics of Income, Treasury data, tax return data, administrative datasets, JCT analyses, GAO reports, or other credible empirical sources.

Where data permit, record measures such as:

- number of returns or taxpayers using the provision;
- amount deducted, excluded, credited, deferred, or otherwise receiving preferential treatment;
- estimated reduction in tax liability;
- income distribution;
- business or industry distribution;
- geographic distribution; and
- changes in use over time.

Eligibility should never be treated as proof of actual use.

### 7. Analyze beneficiaries and economic incidence

"Who benefits?" is an empirical question, not a label inferred solely from the statutory recipient.

The project should distinguish among:

#### Legal eligibility

Who is legally entitled to claim or use the provision?

#### Observed users

Who actually claims or uses it according to available data?

#### Direct tax benefit

Who experiences the immediate reduction, deferral, or other change in tax liability, and how large is that benefit where measurable?

#### Economic incidence

Who ultimately captures the economic value after accounting for behavioral responses, prices, wages, returns to capital, market structure, or other relevant effects?

#### Distribution

How are benefits distributed across income groups, industries, firm sizes, geographies, or other relevant populations?

These categories should not be collapsed into a single unsupported statement.

For example, evidence that 2,343 taxpayers claimed a provision and collectively reduced reported tax liability by a measured amount would be strong evidence about observed use and direct tax benefit. It would not, by itself, establish that those taxpayers captured the entire economic benefit.

Economic incidence may be inferred when credible evidence supports the inference. The record should explain the evidence and the reasoning rather than merely state the conclusion.

Where evidence is incomplete, the project should state what can be established, what is inferred, and what remains unknown.

## Evidence and Confidence

The project should evaluate claims using evidence appropriate to the type of claim rather than relying on one universal source hierarchy.

### Current law

Prefer:

1. U.S. Code and enacted Public Laws;
2. applicable regulations and authoritative administrative guidance;
3. relevant judicial decisions where interpretation depends on case law;
4. official technical explanations; and
5. reliable secondary legal analysis for context.

### Enactment and amendment history

Prefer:

1. enacted Public Laws and historical statutory text;
2. committee and conference reports;
3. contemporaneous JCT materials;
4. Congress.gov, GovInfo, and other official legislative records; and
5. high-quality historical or legal scholarship for context or gaps.

### Stated purpose or rationale

Prefer contemporaneous evidence tied to the legislation.

A committee report explaining a provision is stronger evidence of a documented legislative rationale than a later commentator's explanation of why the provision exists.

Statements by individual legislators should be attributed to those legislators rather than automatically described as the intent of Congress as a whole.

### Fiscal effects

Prefer estimates from JCT, Treasury, CBO, and other official government sources, while preserving the type of estimate, date, budget window, baseline, and important methodological assumptions.

### Use and distribution

Prefer IRS administrative and Statistics of Income data, Treasury and JCT analyses, GAO analyses, and transparent empirical datasets.

### Economic incidence and behavioral effects

Use credible empirical economic research, official economic analyses, and other sources with transparent methodology.

Official status alone does not make a source the best evidence for every economic question. Peer-reviewed or otherwise rigorous empirical research may provide evidence unavailable from statutory and legislative sources.

## Confidence in Claims

Confidence should reflect the quality, directness, consistency, and completeness of the evidence.

The project may use the following evidence states:

- **Established** — directly supported by authoritative or highly reliable evidence with little meaningful ambiguity.
- **Supported** — the available evidence supports the conclusion, but important limitations or inferential steps remain.
- **Uncertain** — evidence exists but is incomplete, indirect, conflicting, or insufficient for a strong conclusion.
- **Disputed** — credible sources reach materially different conclusions.
- **Unknown** — the project has not found sufficient evidence to answer the question.

These states describe confidence in a particular claim. They are not scores or evaluations of the provision itself.

Whenever a claim depends on inference, the record should explain why the inference is being made and what evidence would change the confidence assessment.

## Legislative Intent and Historical Claims

Claims about why a provision exists require particular care.

The project should prefer formulations such as:

> The House Ways and Means Committee stated that the provision was intended to...

or:

> A contemporaneous JCT explanation described the provision as...

over:

> Congress created the provision because...

unless the evidence justifies the broader statement.

Researchers should distinguish at least three categories:

### Documented contemporaneous rationale

An explanation contained in legislative or government material associated with enactment.

### Later interpretation

A subsequent government report, academic work, or other source explaining the provision's purpose or history after enactment.

### Project inference

A conclusion drawn by The Provision Project from the structure, timing, surrounding legislation, or other evidence.

These categories should be labeled rather than blended.

## Fiscal Effect

Fiscal estimates must be presented with enough context to understand what is being measured.

Where available, record:

- estimating organization;
- publication or estimate identifier;
- date of estimate;
- years covered;
- baseline or law assumed;
- whether the figure is a tax expenditure estimate, revenue estimate, distributional estimate, or another measure;
- whether behavioral responses are incorporated;
- interactions with other tax provisions; and
- major methodological limitations disclosed by the source.

A tax expenditure estimate should not be described as the amount the government would necessarily collect if the provision were repealed.

JCT explains that tax expenditure calculations differ from repeal revenue estimates because they generally do not incorporate anticipated behavioral responses, may omit interactions with other federal taxes, and focus on tax liabilities rather than the timing of federal receipts. Estimates for individual tax expenditures also should not automatically be summed because provisions can interact.

When estimates from different years or organizations are compared, changes in law, baselines, economic assumptions, data, and estimating methodology should be considered before interpreting the difference as a real change in fiscal effect.

## Handling Disagreement

The project should not manufacture consensus where credible sources disagree.

When evidence conflicts:

1. identify the competing conclusions;
2. identify the source of each;
3. describe important differences in data, assumptions, population, time period, or methodology;
4. indicate the evidence state; and
5. avoid resolving the disagreement unless the evidence supports doing so.

Arguments from advocacy organizations, industries, affected groups, elected officials, and critics may be useful evidence about the debate surrounding a provision. They should be attributed and should not be presented as independent factual conclusions merely because they are frequently repeated.

## Source Provenance

Important factual and analytical claims should be traceable to their supporting sources.

A provision record should not rely on a bibliography alone when doing so makes it unclear which source supports which claim.

At minimum, source records should preserve:

- title;
- author or issuing institution;
- publication date;
- document identifier when available;
- URL or stable locator;
- relevant page, section, table, or other pinpoint reference where practical;
- date accessed where appropriate; and
- the claim or portion of the record the source supports.

Direct quotations should be used sparingly. The project should generally summarize evidence while preserving enough citation detail for a reader to inspect the original source.

## Separating Evidence from Project Inference

The Provision Project may make technical inferences, but they must be visible as inferences.

A research record should make it possible for a reader to distinguish:

- what the law says;
- what a government source estimates;
- what empirical research finds;
- what a participant in the policy debate argues; and
- what The Provision Project concludes from those materials.

An inference should identify its supporting evidence and confidence level.

## Completion Criteria

A provision record reaches initial research completeness when each required category has been investigated and either answered with sourced evidence or explicitly marked as unresolved.

For the MVP, a provision record should include:

- defined provision boundaries;
- current-law explanation and statutory citations;
- eligibility rules;
- observed use where data are available;
- original enactment and effective date;
- documented rationale or an explicit statement that it could not be established;
- material amendment history;
- analysis of direct beneficiaries and economic incidence, with confidence and limitations;
- fiscal-effect estimates with methodological context;
- significant disagreements or uncertainties;
- claim-level or clearly mapped source provenance; and
- a final source audit.

"Unknown" is an acceptable research result. An unsupported answer is not.

## Final Source Audit

Before a provision record is treated as complete, review it for:

- unsupported factual claims;
- claims of legislative intent that exceed the evidence;
- confusion between eligibility, use, direct tax benefit, and economic incidence;
- fiscal estimates presented without their measurement context;
- outdated estimates presented as current;
- advocacy claims presented as independent facts;
- project inferences presented as sourced conclusions;
- missing primary sources that are reasonably obtainable; and
- citations that do not actually support the claims attached to them.

The purpose of the audit is not to eliminate uncertainty. It is to make the uncertainty, evidence, and reasoning visible.
